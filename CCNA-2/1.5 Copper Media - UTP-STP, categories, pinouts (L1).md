## 3 · TL;DR ⚡

- **Copper twisted‑pair** carries a **differential electrical** signal over four **twisted pairs** in a jacket. It reaches **~100 m**, is **cheap**, is the **universal access medium**, and **carries PoE** (`1.2`) — but is **EMI‑prone** and distance‑limited (fiber, `1.6`, is the long/immune option).
- 🔑 **Twisting is noise cancellation built into the geometry.** Data is sent as a **voltage difference** across a pair; external noise hits **both** wires equally and cancels in the difference — and twisting ensures each wire spends equal time in every position so **crosstalk and EMI cancel**. Tighter twists / higher category = better cancellation = more usable bandwidth.
- **Shielding:** **UTP** (unshielded — the common case) vs **STP/FTP/S‑FTP** (foil/braid — for electrically noisy environments; needs proper grounding).
- **Categories climb in bandwidth → speed:** **Cat5e** (1 G / 100 m) → **Cat6** (10 G only to ~55 m) → **Cat6A** (10 G / 100 m — the modern standard) → **Cat7** (niche) → **Cat8** (25/40 G, but only ~30 m — DC short runs). **All ~100 m** except Cat8.
- **Terminated on RJ‑45** with a **pinout**: **T568A** vs **T568B** are the same electrically, just a **wire‑order convention** (A↔B swap the green/orange pairs) — **pick one and be consistent**. **Straight‑through** (same standard both ends) connects **unlike** devices; **crossover** (A/B) connects **like** devices — but **Auto‑MDIX** now makes the straight/crossover choice moot.
- 🔑 **Gauge matters:** thicker conductors (**23 AWG** in Cat6/6A) have **less resistance** than thin (**26 AWG**) → **less loss and heat**, and **better PoE** (`1.2`). **Solid** cable for permanent runs; **stranded** for flexible patch cords.
- **The ~100 m limit** comes from **attenuation** (the signal fades) plus Ethernet timing — go farther and you need fiber or an intermediate switch.

## 4 · Importance — the problem first 🎯

**The problem:** you need to carry a **fast digital signal** from a device to a switch, **reliably**, over a useful distance, **through an electrically hostile world**, and **cheaply enough** to run to every desk, camera, and access point in a building. Each of those constraints fights you:

1. **The signal fades over distance (attenuation).** Every metre of copper saps the signal; go too far and the receiver can't tell a 1 from a 0. → a hard **~100 m** reach, and a reason **cable quality/gauge** matter.
2. **The world is full of electrical noise (EMI/crosstalk).** Motors, fluorescent lights, power cables — and the cable's own neighbouring pairs — induce interference. Raw parallel wires would be unusable. → the pairs are **twisted** (and sometimes **shielded**) to **cancel** that noise.
3. **Speed keeps rising.** 100 Mbps became 1 G became 10 G. The **same RJ‑45 cable** must carry ever‑higher frequencies without the pairs interfering. → **categories** define how much bandwidth a cable can carry (tighter twists, better isolation).
4. **It must be cheap and touch‑friendly, and often carry power.** Copper is inexpensive, terminates with a simple crimp, and — uniquely — can carry **PoE** (`1.2`). → it's the **default access medium** for endpoints, phones, APs, and cameras.

So copper cabling is where all the fast silicon of `1.1` either connects to the world **reliably and affordably… or doesn't**. The cable is **part of the circuit**, not a passive afterthought: choose the wrong **category** (Cat5e for a 10 G link), the wrong **shielding** (UTP through heavy EMI), the wrong **gauge** (thin cable for a long PoE run), or a **wrong pinout**, and the link errors, under‑performs, or won't come up at all — the exact faults you'll diagnose in `1.8`.

🔵 **Why the depth?** Because the most common physical‑layer questions — *why is this link slow / erroring / not linking* — often trace to a **copper choice**: too‑long or too‑thin cable, EMI on unshielded runs, a category that can't carry the speed, or a mis‑wired connector. Understanding **why twisting works, what a category rating means, and how gauge affects loss/PoE** is what lets you pick and diagnose copper correctly.

## 5 · Description 📋

**Twisted pair** 🟢 — four **twisted pairs** of copper in a jacket; twisting cancels **EMI and crosstalk**.
- **UTP** (Unshielded Twisted Pair) — no shield; the common case.
- **STP / FTP / S‑FTP** — **Shielded / Foiled** twisted pair; a foil or braid per‑pair and/or overall, for **noisy environments** (needs proper grounding).

**Categories** 🟢 (bandwidth → speed): **Cat5e → Cat6 → Cat6A → Cat7 → Cat8** (details in §8).

**Connector & pinout** 🟢:
- **RJ‑45** (8P8C) — the standard 8‑pin copper connector.
- **T568A / T568B** — two **wire‑order** standards (electrically identical); A↔B swap the green/orange pairs.
- **Straight‑through** (same standard both ends) vs **crossover** (A one end, B the other).
- **Auto‑MDIX** — the port auto‑detects and internally swaps, so straight/crossover no longer matters.

**Construction** 🔵:
- **Gauge (AWG)** — conductor thickness; **23 AWG** (Cat6/6A) is thicker/lower‑resistance than **24–26 AWG** (Cat5e).
- **Solid vs stranded** — **solid** for permanent in‑wall runs (lower attenuation); **stranded** for flexible **patch cords**.

**Legacy** 🔵 — **coaxial (coax)** — old bus/thicknet‑thinnet Ethernet; today mainly cable‑modem/DOCSIS access.

## 6 · How It Works — the physics & the wiring ⚙️

### 6.1 Twisting, differential signalling & crosstalk 🔑
```
A pair carries a DIFFERENTIAL signal: the receiver reads the VOLTAGE DIFFERENCE between the two wires.
External noise (EMI) couples into BOTH wires nearly equally → in the difference, it CANCELS (common-mode rejection).
TWISTING makes each wire spend equal time in each position → the coupling stays balanced → noise cancels,
and adjacent pairs interfere less (CROSSTALK drops). Tighter twist rate + better isolation = higher category.
SHIELDING (F/UTP, S/FTP) adds a foil/braid to reject severe external EMI (must be grounded to work).
```

### 6.2 Category = how much bandwidth the cable carries 🟢
A higher **category** = a higher **frequency (MHz) rating** = more data rate over the same 100 m, achieved through **tighter twists, better pair isolation, and (for higher cats) shielding**. This is why the same RJ‑45 cable can be 1 G (Cat5e) or 10 G (Cat6A).

### 6.3 Gauge, loss & PoE 🔵
Thicker conductors (**23 AWG**) have **less resistance** than thin (**26 AWG**) → **less signal loss and heat** over a run, and **better PoE** (more power delivered, less waste as heat — `1.2`). Longer + thinner = more loss.

### 6.4 The pinout: T568A/B, straight vs crossover, Auto‑MDIX 🟢
```
T568A and T568B are electrically identical — just a WIRE-ORDER convention (A↔B swap green & orange pairs).
Pick ONE standard site-wide and stay consistent.

STRAIGHT-THROUGH (same standard both ends): connects UNLIKE devices — PC↔switch, switch↔router.
CROSSOVER (A one end, B the other):          connects LIKE devices — switch↔switch, PC↔PC, router↔router.

10/100 Mbps uses only pairs on pins 1,2 and 3,6. GIGABIT uses ALL FOUR pairs.
MDI (end devices) vs MDI-X (switches/hubs) decided who "crossed" internally.
AUTO-MDIX: the port auto-detects and swaps internally → straight-vs-crossover no longer matters (use straight).
```

### 6.5 The ~100 m limit 🟢
Copper **attenuates** the signal per metre, and Ethernet has timing constraints; together they cap a copper run at **~100 m** (Cat8 is even shorter, ~30 m, because it pushes far higher frequencies). Beyond that → **fiber** (`1.6`) or an intermediate switch.

### 6.6 ⚡ Copper relationships (the "why") 🔑
> The cable is part of the circuit; these relationships decide which copper.

**① Twisting/category ↔ crosstalk & bandwidth.** More/tighter twists and better isolation = better noise cancellation = higher usable frequency = more speed over the same 100 m. *Copper speed is a manufacturing‑quality relationship — the category rating is that quality.*

**② Shielding ↔ EMI environment.** **UTP** is fine in normal environments; **STP/FTP** is for **electrically noisy** ones (near motors, power, industrial gear) — but shielding **must be grounded** or it can make things worse. *Add shielding for the environment, not by default — and ground it.*

**③ Gauge ↔ loss, heat & PoE.** Thicker (23 AWG) = less resistance = less loss/heat and **better PoE** delivery; thinner (26 AWG) loses more. *The conductor thickness is part of the power and signal budget, not just mechanics* (§6.3; `1.2`).

**④ Category ↔ speed vs distance.** Cat6 does 10 G only to ~55 m; **Cat6A** does 10 G to 100 m; **Cat8** does 25/40 G but only ~30 m. *Higher speed on copper costs distance — read the speed‑at‑distance, not just the headline rate.*

**⑤ Solid vs stranded ↔ runs vs patch cords.** **Solid** has lower attenuation → permanent **in‑wall runs**; **stranded** is flexible → **patch cords**. *Use the right build for the job — stranded in long runs loses more; solid in a flexed patch cord fatigues.*

**⑥ T568A vs T568B ↔ consistency, not correctness.** They're **electrically identical**; the only rule is **be consistent** site‑wide. Mixing them by accident on a cable creates an unintended **crossover**. *The standard you pick doesn't matter; picking one and sticking to it does.*

**⑦ Straight vs crossover ↔ device type (now automated).** Historically **like** devices needed a **crossover**; **Auto‑MDIX** removed the distinction by swapping internally. *On modern gear, always use straight‑through and let Auto‑MDIX handle it.*

**⑧ 100 m ↔ attenuation + timing.** The reach cap is physics (fading) plus Ethernet timing — and it **shrinks** as speed/frequency rises (Cat8 ~30 m). *Distance and speed trade against each other on copper; past the limit, go fiber* (`1.6`).

## 7 · Pros & Cons ⚖️

| Aspect | ✅ Strength | ⚠️ Trade‑off |
|---|---|---|
| **Copper (UTP)** | Cheap; easy to terminate; universal access medium | EMI‑prone; ≤100 m; bandwidth‑capped vs fiber |
| **Carries PoE** | Powers APs/phones/cameras (`1.2`) | Gauge/heat must be managed |
| **UTP** | Cheapest; fine in normal environments | No EMI shielding |
| **STP/FTP** | Rejects severe EMI | Costlier; **must be grounded**; stiffer |
| **Higher category** | More speed (up to 25/40 G) | Higher speed = shorter distance (Cat8 ~30 m) |
| **Solid** | Lower attenuation (runs) | Not flexible |
| **Stranded** | Flexible (patch cords) | More attenuation |

## 8 · Variants — categories, shielding, builds 🌿

**Categories:**

| Cat | Bandwidth | Speed @ distance | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cat5e** | 100 MHz | 1 G @ 100 m | baseline; still common |
| **Cat6** | 250 MHz | 1 G @100 m · **10 G @ ~55 m** | 10 G only on short runs |
| **Cat6A** | 500 MHz | **10 G @ 100 m** | 10 G standard for new work |
| **Cat7** | 600 MHz | 10 G (shielded, non‑RJ45 variants) | niche/marketing |
| **Cat8** | 2000 MHz | **25/40 G @ ~30 m** | data‑center short runs |

**Shielding types:** **U/UTP** (unshielded) · **F/UTP** (overall foil) · **S/FTP** (braided screen + per‑pair foil) — increasing EMI rejection and cost.

**Pinout:** **T568A / T568B** (wire order) · **straight‑through / crossover** (device type) · **Auto‑MDIX** (automatic).

**Build:** **solid** (runs) vs **stranded** (patch cords); **gauge** 23 AWG (Cat6/6A) vs 24–26 AWG (Cat5e).

**Legacy:** **coax** (thicknet/thinnet Ethernet → now DOCSIS/cable‑modem).

## 10 · Best Practices ✅

- **Install Cat6A for new copper** so you have a 10 G path to 100 m; reserve **Cat8** for short DC runs (§8, §6.6 ④).
- **Use UTP by default; add shielding (STP/FTP) only for genuinely noisy environments** — and **ground it** (§6.6 ②).
- **Match gauge to the run** — thicker (23 AWG) for long/PoE runs to cut loss and heat (§6.6 ③; `1.2`).
- **Pick one pinout standard (A or B) site‑wide and stay consistent** (§6.6 ⑥).
- **Use straight‑through cables and rely on Auto‑MDIX** on modern gear (§6.6 ⑦).
- **Solid for permanent runs, stranded for patch cords** (§6.6 ⑤).
- **Respect the ~100 m limit** — beyond it, use fiber (`1.6`) or an intermediate switch (§6.5).
- **Label and test terminations** (a cable tester verifies pinout/continuity) to avoid the faults you'd chase in `1.8`.

## 11 · No‑Goes 🚫

- ❌ **Expecting 10 G over Cat6 at 100 m.** Cat6 does 10 G only to ~55 m; use **Cat6A** (§6.6 ④).
- ❌ **Running copper past 100 m.** Attenuation kills it — use fiber or a switch in between (§6.5).
- ❌ **Unshielded copper through heavy EMI** (next to motors/power) — errors and instability; shield or go fiber (§6.6 ②).
- ❌ **Shielded cable left ungrounded** — can perform worse than UTP (§6.6 ②).
- ❌ **Mixing T568A and T568B on one cable by accident** — creates an unintended crossover (§6.6 ⑥).
- ❌ **Thin/long cable for high‑power PoE** — more loss, heat, and instability (§6.6 ③; `1.2`).
- ❌ **Stranded cable for long permanent runs** — higher attenuation; use solid (§6.6 ⑤).
- ❌ **Covering fiber or shared‑vs‑p2p here** — those are `1.6` and `1.7`.

## 13 · Practical Tasks 🧪

1. **Read the print** 🟢 — on a real cable, read the category and shielding markings (Cat5e/6/6A, U/UTP vs S/FTP) and note the gauge if printed (§5, §8).
2. **Category vs speed/distance** 🔑 — reason about a 10 G link over Cat6 at 80 m vs Cat6A at 80 m: which links, which errors, and why (§6.6 ④).
3. **Wire a pinout** 🟢 — lay out T568B pin order from memory; explain what changes for T568A and when you'd use a crossover (and why Auto‑MDIX makes it moot) (§6.4, §6.6 ⑥⑦).
4. **Twisting reasoning** 🔵 — explain in your own words why differential signalling + twisting cancels EMI, and why tighter twists raise the category (§6.1, §6.6 ①).
5. **Gauge & PoE** 🔵 — for a 90 m PoE run, argue 23 AWG Cat6A vs 26 AWG Cat5e on loss, heat, and deliverable power (§6.3, §6.6 ③; `1.2`).
6. **Test a cable** 🟢 — use a cable tester to verify pinout/continuity on a crimped cable; identify a mis‑wire (sets up `1.8`) (§10).
