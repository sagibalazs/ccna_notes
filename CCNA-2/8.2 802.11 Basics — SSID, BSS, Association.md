## 3. ⚡ TL;DR

- **BSS** = one AP + its clients (the basic cell). **BSSID** = that AP radio's **48-bit MAC** — the on-air *address*. **SSID** = the human-readable *name* (≤ 32 chars, case-sensitive).
- **ESS** = multiple BSSs sharing **one SSID**, joined by a **Distribution System (DS)** → lets clients **roam** between APs seamlessly.
- **IBSS** = ad hoc, peer-to-peer, **no AP** (rare; doesn't scale).
- A client **joins in 3 steps**: **Scan** (passive = hear beacons / active = probe) → **Authenticate** (legacy Open System, a null handshake) → **Associate** (exchange capabilities, get an AID). *Then* data flows.
- 🚨 **802.11 "authentication" is NOT security.** Open System authenticates *everyone*. Real security (WPA2/3) is a separate 4-way handshake *after* association.
- **Beacons** advertise the BSS every **100 TU (102.4 ms)** — *1 TU = 1024 µs, not 1 ms*.
- Three 802.11 frame types: **management** (build the connection), **control** (govern the medium), **data** (carry payload).

---

## 4. 🎯 Importance — The Problem First

**The problem:** In 8.1 we established that the air is a *shared, nameless, address-less* medium. A radio switched on in a room hears… energy. For that energy to become a *network* a client can find and join, three things have to be invented that a cable gives you for free:

1. **Identity — "which network is this?"** On a wire, you plug into the one network the cable leads to. In the air, a dozen networks overlap in the same space. Each needs a **name** clients can recognize (SSID) and a hardware **address** frames can target (BSSID).

2. **Membership — "am I allowed on, and does the AP know about me?"** A switch port implicitly "admits" whatever you plug in. Wireless has no plug. A client must *explicitly ask to join* and the AP must *explicitly track it* (buffer its frames, assign it an ID, apply its policy). That's what association is.

3. **Discovery & mobility — "what's available, and can I move?"** Ethernet devices don't wander between ports mid-session. Wireless clients walk down hallways. The network must let a client **discover** APs it can't see, and **hand off** from one AP to the next without dropping the session.

802.11's answer to all three is the **service-set model** (BSS/ESS/SSID/BSSID) plus the **scan→auth→associate** handshake. This is the machinery behind every "connecting…" spinner you've ever watched.

Why an engineer must know it cold:
- **Troubleshooting lives here.** "Client won't connect," "keeps dropping," "connects but no data," "roaming is slow" — every one of these is a specific failure at a specific step of the state machine. If you don't know the steps, you're guessing.
- **Security starts here.** Deauth attacks, evil-twin/rogue APs, hidden-SSID myths, the WPA handshake — all of them attach to this handshake. You can't reason about wireless security without the join sequence in your head.
- **Design decisions ride on it.** How many SSIDs per AP? One VLAN per SSID? Beacon overhead? Roaming domains? These are BSS/ESS questions.

> 🕵️ **Dirty secret:** The most-tested and most-*misunderstood* fact in wireless: the word "authentication" appears **twice** and means **two completely different things**. The 802.11 *Open System authentication* in step ② is a fossil — a null handshake that lets literally anyone through. The *actual* security check (are you allowed on CorpNet?) happens **later**, after association, as WPA2/WPA3. Confusing these two is the classic beginner mistake.

## 5. 📖 Description

**The service-set vocabulary** 🟢:

- **BSS (Basic Service Set)** — the fundamental building block: **one AP** and all the client stations (STAs) associated to it. In a BSS, clients **never talk directly** to each other — every frame is relayed *through the AP*.
- **BSSID (Basic Service Set Identifier)** — the **48-bit MAC address** of the AP's radio that identifies the BSS at Layer 2. It's the *address* frames use. (When one AP hosts several SSIDs, each gets its **own BSSID**, on Cisco derived by incrementing the radio's base MAC.)
- **BSA (Basic Service Area)** — the physical coverage footprint of the BSS. Also called the **cell**. Its size depends on Tx power, antenna gain, and surroundings (all from 8.1).
- **SSID (Service Set Identifier)** — the **network name**: up to **32 characters**, **case-sensitive**. Human-facing. It is *not* an address — it's a label.
- **ESS (Extended Service Set)** — **two or more BSSs** sharing the **same SSID**, joined by a common **Distribution System**. This is what almost every real enterprise/home network is: several APs presenting *one* logical network.
- **ESSID** — the SSID as used across an ESS; in practice **synonymous with SSID**.
- **DS (Distribution System)** — the (usually **wired Ethernet**) backbone that interconnects the APs of an ESS so traffic can leave the wireless cell and clients can roam.
- **IBSS (Independent BSS)** — **ad hoc / peer-to-peer** mode with **no AP**; STAs talk directly. Doesn't scale past ~8–10 devices; today mostly a security curiosity (WIPS flags it as possible rogue activity).

**Infrastructure mode vs ad hoc:** Infrastructure = clients connect *via an AP* (BSS/ESS). Ad hoc = clients connect *directly* (IBSS). CCNA is 99% infrastructure mode.

**The three 802.11 frame types** 🟢:

- **Management frames** — *build and maintain* the connection: **beacon, probe request/response, authentication, association request/response, reassociation, disassociation, deauthentication.**
- **Control frames** — *govern medium access & delivery*: **RTS, CTS, ACK, Block ACK.** (These are how half-duplex radios take turns — the CSMA/CA plumbing.)
- **Data frames** — *carry the actual payload* (the upper-layer packet from the client).

## 6. 🔧 How It Works — Under the Hood

### 6.1 Step ①: Scanning — how a client discovers APs 🟢

A client can't join what it can't find. Two discovery methods:

**Passive scanning** — the client **listens**. It parks on a channel and waits to overhear **Beacon** frames, which every AP broadcasts continuously.
```
   AP ── Beacon ──► (broadcast, ~every 102.4 ms, to everyone in range)
   STA: "I heard CorpNet on ch6, BSSID ...00:99, RSSI −58 dBm. Noted."
   STA hops to next channel, repeats.
```
- Pro: client stays silent (good for battery/stealth). Con: **slower** — must wait a full beacon interval on *every* channel.

**Active scanning** — the client **asks**. It transmits a **Probe Request** ("anyone here? / is CorpNet here?"); APs answer with a **Probe Response** (same content as a beacon, unicast to the asker).
```
   STA ── Probe Request ──►   ("null SSID" = any network, or a named SSID)
   AP  ◄── Probe Response ──   (SSID, rates, capabilities, security…)
```
- Pro: **faster**, lower latency/power per channel. Con: client reveals itself (and, if probing by name, **leaks the SSIDs it remembers** — a privacy issue).

The **beacon** and **probe response** carry the same essentials: **Timestamp** (for time sync, 8 B), **Beacon Interval** (2 B), **Capability Info** (2 B), **SSID** (variable, ≤32), **Supported Rates**, channel, security parameters, and the **TIM/DTIM** power-save map.

### 6.2 Beacon timing — the 100 TU / 1024 µs trap 🟢

Beacons fire at the **TBTT (Target Beacon Transmission Time)**:
```
   Beacon interval (default) = 100 TU
   1 TU  = 1024 microseconds   ⟵  NOT 1 millisecond!
   100 TU = 100 × 1024 µs = 102,400 µs = 102.4 ms  (≈ 9.8 beacons/sec)
```
"Target" because if the medium is busy at the TBTT (CSMA/CA), the beacon waits its turn — it's a goal, not a guarantee.

> 🕵️ **Dirty secret:** Nearly every casual source says "beacons every 100 ms." Wrong by 2.4%. **1 TU = 1024 µs**, so it's **102.4 ms**. The IEEE chose 1024 (2¹⁰) because timers are binary. Small thing — but it's exactly the kind of "did you actually read the standard?" detail that separates memorized answers from understanding.

### 6.3 Step ②: 802.11 Authentication (the legacy null step) 🟢

Once the client picks an AP (usually strongest RSSI for the desired SSID), it sends an **Authentication Request**; the AP returns an **Authentication Response**. Two frames, done.
```
   STA ── Auth Request ──►    (Open System: "auth algorithm = 0")
   AP  ◄── Auth Response ──   ("status = success")   ✅ always, basically
```
- **Open System authentication** is a **null algorithm** — it authenticates *anyone who asks*. It exists purely to satisfy the state machine's shape.
- (The old **Shared Key authentication** used WEP and is **deprecated/insecure** — never used today. 🔵)
- **Real security is NOT here.** WPA2/WPA3 runs *after* association (§6.6).

### 6.4 Step ③: Association 🟢

Now the client formally **joins** the BSS:
```
   STA ── Association Request ──►  (my capabilities: rates, channels,
                                    security suite, power-save mode…)
   AP  ◄── Association Response ──  (accepted + AID, or rejected + reason)
```
- The AP evaluates the client's capabilities, and if compatible, **accepts** and assigns an **AID (Association Identifier)** — a small number used to track the client (e.g. for buffering its traffic during power-save via the TIM).
- After a *successful* association on an **open** network, **data frames can flow immediately**.
- On a **secured** network, the client is associated but **blocked from data** until it clears the security handshake (§6.6).

### 6.5 The state machine, formally 🟢🔵

```
  ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ STATE 1: Unauthenticated, Unassociated         │  ← only mgmt/scan frames
  └───────────────────┬────────────────────────────┘
        802.11 auth ✔ │
  ┌───────────────────▼────────────────────────────┐
  │ STATE 2: Authenticated, Unassociated           │
  └───────────────────┬────────────────────────────┘
        association ✔ │
  ┌───────────────────▼────────────────────────────┐
  │ STATE 3: Authenticated, Associated             │  ← open net: data OK
  └───────────────────┬────────────────────────────┘
        802.1X/WPA ✔  │  (modern RSNA only)
  ┌───────────────────▼────────────────────────────┐
  │ STATE 4: RSNA-Authenticated (data allowed) 🔒  │  ← secured net: data OK
  └────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   Falling back: Disassociation → State 2 ; Deauthentication → State 1
```
- **Disassociation** drops you from associated → authenticated (leave the BSS).
- **Deauthentication** drops you all the way to unauthenticated (kicked out entirely).
- Both are *notifications*, not requests — and (pre-802.11w) **unprotected**, which is why the **deauth flood** attack works (covered in the Security block).

### 6.6 Where real security fits (preview of 8.6) 🔒

```
  … Association Response ✔
        │
        ▼
   802.1X / EAP  (enterprise)   OR   PSK (personal)
        │
        ▼
   WPA2/WPA3 4-WAY HANDSHAKE  → derive encryption keys
        │
        ▼
   🔒 encrypted data frames flow
```
The takeaway for now: **association ≠ authorized.** The client is "on the cell" but the gate to actual data is the *separate* WPA handshake. Full detail in **8.6**.

### 6.7 Multiple SSIDs, VLANs & BSSIDs on one AP 🟢🔵

A single physical AP radio can advertise several logical networks:
```
   ┌──────────── one AP radio (base MAC aa:bb:cc:00:00) ───────────┐
   │ SSID "Corp"   → BSSID …00:01  → VLAN 10                        │
   │ SSID "Guest"  → BSSID …00:02  → VLAN 20                        │
   │ SSID "IoT"    → BSSID …00:03  → VLAN 30                        │
   └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```
- Each SSID gets its **own BSSID** (Cisco increments the radio MAC) and typically maps to **one VLAN** — this is how one AP separates Corp / Guest / IoT traffic.
- **Cost:** every SSID beacons independently → more airtime spent on management overhead. Best practice: **≤ 3–4 SSIDs per radio.** More SSIDs = less airtime for actual data.

### 6.8 Hidden SSID — the security-theater myth 🕵️

Some deployments enable "SSID cloaking": the AP sends beacons with an **empty/null SSID** field so the name doesn't show in a casual scan.

Why it's **not real security**:
- The SSID still travels **in the clear** in **probe requests, probe responses, and association frames** — anyone sniffing captures it in seconds.
- Worse, it forces your **clients** to actively probe for the network *by name* wherever they go — so your laptops broadcast "is CorpNet here?" in every coffee shop, *leaking* the name and aiding tracking.
- It breaks/degrades roaming and some clients. Net effect: **more risk, more hassle, near-zero security gain.**

## 7. ⚖️ Pros & Cons

**Infrastructure mode (BSS/ESS)**
✅ Central control, roaming, scales to thousands of clients · ✅ AP relays = clients needn't hear each other · ✅ Maps cleanly to wired VLANs
❌ Single AP = single point of failure for its cell · ❌ All cell traffic funnels through the AP (half-duplex airtime)

**Ad hoc (IBSS)**
✅ No infrastructure needed, instant peer-to-peer · ✅ Useful for quick device-to-device
❌ Doesn't scale (~8–10) · ❌ No central security/management · ❌ Flagged as rogue/security risk

**Active vs passive scanning**
✅ Active = fast discovery / lower latency · ❌ Active = client reveals itself & leaks remembered SSIDs
✅ Passive = silent / private · ❌ Passive = slower (must wait a beacon interval per channel)

**More SSIDs per AP**
✅ Segments users/VLANs on shared hardware · ❌ Each SSID adds beacon overhead → less data airtime

## 8. 🔀 Variants (Common → Specific)

- **Topologies:** Infrastructure (**BSS** → **ESS**) → **IBSS** (ad hoc) → **MBSS** (mesh — APs bridge to each other wirelessly instead of via a wired DS) → **PBSS** (802.11ad 60 GHz personal BSS). 🔵
- **Scanning:** Passive (beacon-listen) vs Active (probe). 🟢
- **802.11 authentication algorithms:** **Open System** (null, universal) vs **Shared Key** (WEP, deprecated). 🔵
- **Association events:** **Association** (first join) vs **Reassociation** (roam to a new AP in the same ESS) vs **Disassociation** / **Deauthentication** (leave/kicked). 🟢
- **SSID visibility:** Broadcast (normal) vs Hidden/cloaked (null SSID in beacon). 🟢

## 9. 🧩 Devices / Media / Protocols

- **AP (Access Point)** — creates the BSS, sends beacons, relays all client traffic, hosts the SSID(s)/BSSID(s).
- **STA (Station / client)** — any Wi-Fi device (laptop, phone, IoT) that scans, authenticates, associates.
- **WLC (Wireless LAN Controller)** — in enterprise, orchestrates many APs into one ESS, manages roaming and (in split-MAC) the association state (→ 8.3).
- **DS (Distribution System)** — the wired switch/LAN backbone stitching BSSs into an ESS.
- **Frames/protocols:** 802.11 **management** (beacon/probe/auth/assoc/deauth), **control** (RTS/CTS/ACK), **data**. **CSMA/CA** underlies medium access. **802.1X/EAP + WPA2/3** provide the real post-association security (→ 8.6).
- **Tools:** Wireshark (filter `wlan.fc.type_subtype` to watch the join live), any Wi-Fi scanner to list SSID/BSSID/channel/RSSI.

## 10. ✅ Best Practices

- **One SSID across all APs in a roaming domain** (an ESS) — same SSID, *different* BSSID per AP — so clients roam without reconnecting.
- **Keep SSIDs to ≤ 3–4 per radio.** Each one costs beacon airtime; six SSIDs on 2.4 GHz can waste a shocking fraction of the channel.
- **Map each SSID to one VLAN** for clean segmentation (Corp/Guest/IoT).
- **Don't rely on hidden SSIDs for security** — use WPA2/WPA3 (→ 8.6). Cloaking adds hassle, not protection.
- **Use meaningful but not sensitive SSID names** (don't embed the company's internal secrets or unit numbers).
- **Leave beacon interval at 100 TU** unless you have a specific power-save/roaming reason — lowering floods the air, raising hurts discovery/roaming.
- **Design the ESS for RSSI overlap** (~15–20% cell overlap) so roaming clients always have a next AP above the roaming threshold.

## 11. 🚫 No-Goes & Troubleshooting Traps

- ❌ **Confusing SSID with BSSID.** SSID = *name* (can be shared by many APs); BSSID = *MAC address* (unique per AP radio/SSID). Frames use the BSSID.
- ❌ **Thinking 802.11 Open System auth = security.** It authenticates everyone. Security is the separate WPA handshake after association.
- ❌ **Using different SSIDs per AP** for one logical network → breaks seamless roaming (client sees them as separate networks).
- ❌ **Piling on SSIDs** → management-frame overhead starves data throughput.
- ❌ **Trusting hidden SSID** → the name leaks in probes/assoc frames and makes clients broadcast it everywhere.
- ❌ **Assuming "associated" = "working."** On secured nets, a client can be associated (State 3) yet blocked at the WPA/802.1X gate (no data). "Connected, no internet" often lives here.
- ❌ **Saying "beacons every 100 ms."** It's **102.4 ms** (1 TU = 1024 µs).

**Troubleshooting map — "client won't connect / drops":**
```
   Won't even see network?     → scan/beacon issue: hidden SSID? wrong band/channel? out of range (RSSI)?
   Sees it, won't auth?        → 802.11 auth rarely fails on open; if WPA, it's really the 4-way handshake → wrong PSK / 802.1X / RADIUS
   Auths, won't associate?     → capability/rate mismatch, AP client-limit, MAC filter, AID exhaustion
   Associates, no data?        → security gate (State 3→4): WPA handshake / 802.1X / VLAN / DHCP
   Connects then drops?        → deauth/disassoc (roaming, interference, DFS radar hit, or deauth attack)
```

## 13. 🛠️ Practical Tasks (expanded later in context)

1. **Wireshark join capture:** put a Wi-Fi adapter in **monitor mode**, capture, and filter `wlan.fc.type_subtype`. Isolate one client joining and identify each frame in order: **beacon → probe req → probe resp → auth → assoc req → assoc resp**. Confirm the AID in the association response.
2. **Beacon dissection:** open a captured **beacon** and locate the mandatory fields — Timestamp, **Beacon Interval** (read it in TU and convert to ms), Capability Info, SSID, Supported Rates, TIM. Prove to yourself 100 TU = 102.4 ms.
3. **BSSID vs SSID:** in a scanner, find one SSID advertised by two APs — note the **two different BSSIDs** under one shared SSID (that's an ESS). Then find one AP advertising multiple SSIDs — note the **incrementing BSSIDs**.
4. **Active-scan leak:** capture **probe requests** from your own phone with Wi-Fi on but not connected — watch it broadcast the names of networks it remembers. (This is the hidden-SSID privacy problem, live.)
5. **Packet Tracer 8:** build one AP + two laptops on one SSID; then add a second AP with the **same SSID** on a different channel and move a laptop between them to observe re-association (PT abstracts the frames, so use it to drill SSID/VLAN mapping and roaming *concept*).
6. **Deauth observation (lab only, your own gear):** watch a normal disconnect and identify the **deauthentication** frame — the same frame a deauth attack abuses. Sets up the Security block.

But an enterprise has *hundreds* of APs that must share channels (8.1), present one seamless ESS (8.2), and hand clients off cleanly. **Who coordinates them all?** That's the architecture question: a fat **autonomous** AP that does everything itself, a **cloud**-managed model, or the **split-MAC** controller model where a WLC centralizes the brains and the AP becomes a radio. The beacons, probes, and associations from this document are exactly the functions that get *split* between AP and controller next.
