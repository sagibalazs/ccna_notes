## 2 · Visualization — Architecture Blueprint 🗺️

**A WLC is a purpose‑built server running IOS XE.** Many APs tunnel into it over CAPWAP; it manages them all, and — in central switching — their client traffic flows *through* it to the wired VLANs.

```
        📡 AP    📡 AP    📡 AP    📡 AP   … (hundreds/thousands)
          │        │        │        │
          └── CAPWAP control (UDP 5246, DTLS) ──┐   ← WLC manages every AP
          └── CAPWAP data    (UDP 5247) ────────┤   ← client traffic (central switching only)
                                                ▼
   ┌──────────────────────── WIRELESS LAN CONTROLLER ───────────────────────┐
   │  CONTROL PLANE  (the reason it exists):                                 │
   │   • AP join/config   • authentication   • RRM (channel/power)           │
   │   • roaming / mobility database   • security (aWIPS)   • policy/QoS      │
   │                                                                          │
   │  COMPUTE  (borrowed from 1.1.2):  CPU + RAM + storage + IOS XE           │
   │                                                                          │
   │  DATA PLANE  (central switching):  de-encapsulate CAPWAP → bridge onto   │
   │              the wired VLAN   →   THROUGHPUT LIMIT lives here (§9.4 ①)    │
   │                                                                          │
   │  PORTS:  data/uplink (hot-swap 10/40/100GE) ── bundled as LAG ──┐        │
   │          + Redundancy Port (RP) ── SSO ──┐   + console + mgmt + USB (OOB)│
   └──────────────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────┼─────────┘
                                               │                    │
                        ┌──────────────────────▼──┐          🔁 to the wired
                        │  STANDBY WLC (SSO 1:1)   │          distribution/core
                        │  hot state sync; takes   │
                        │  over on failure         │
                        └──────────────────────────┘

CONTROL = manage the APs (always)      DATA = carry client traffic (only in central switching)
```

## 3 · TL;DR ⚡

- A **WLC** is the **central brain for lightweight APs**. Without it, every AP is an island you'd configure by hand with no coordinated RF or roaming. With it, APs become **thin radios** and the controller handles **auth, channel/power (RRM), roaming, security, and policy**.
- Hardware‑wise a WLC is a **server (`1.1.2`) running IOS XE** with fast **data ports** (`1.1.1` NIC atom) bundled into a **LAG**, a dedicated **redundancy port** for **SSO**, and **OOB** console/mgmt.
- **Two switching modes decide whether client data touches the WLC:**
  - **Central switching (local mode):** all client traffic tunnels to the WLC → the WLC's **throughput is a hard aggregate ceiling**.
  - **FlexConnect (local switching):** the AP bridges client traffic locally → the WLC does **control only**, and its throughput barely matters.
- 🔑 **The WLC archetype: throughput ↔ switching mode.** A 9800‑L's "**5 Gbps**" bites *only in central switching*; in FlexConnect the same box quietly handles far more clients because their data never flows through it. *The headline throughput is a limit only when you route data through the brain.*
- **Scale is gated by licensing, not just silicon:** the 9800‑L does **250 APs** base, **500** with a Performance license — same hardware, a license unlocks the ceiling.
- **Redundancy:** **SSO 1:1** (a hot standby mirrors state, takes over instantly) plus **N+1** (one backup for several). Redundancy costs capacity — a standby WLC does nothing until it's needed.
- **Form‑factor descent:** 9800‑80 appliance → 9800‑40 → 9800‑L → 9800‑CL (virtual) → embedded‑on‑switch → **EWC** (an AP hosting the controller) → **Meraki cloud** (no on‑prem controller at all).

## 4 · Importance — the problem first 🎯

**The problem:** one autonomous AP is fine. **Five hundred** autonomous APs are a nightmare — each hand‑configured, each picking its own channel/power blind to its neighbors, none coordinating when a client walks from one to the next. At scale, three things break that a single AP never has to solve:

1. **Configuration doesn't scale by hand.** 500 APs × dozens of settings each = unmanageable and error‑prone. → a controller pushes **one policy** to all APs (**zero‑touch** AP join).
2. **RF is a shared, self‑interfering resource.** If every AP picks its own channel/power, they stomp on each other. Someone must see the **whole RF picture** and assign channels/power globally. → **RRM (Radio Resource Management)** on the WLC.
3. **Clients move.** A phone walking down a hallway must hop AP→AP **without dropping the call or re‑authenticating.** That requires a **central place that remembers the client's state** (IP, auth, VLAN). → the WLC's **mobility/roaming database**.

And a fourth, security: rogue APs and wireless attacks need **network‑wide** detection (**aWIPS**), which only a central controller can correlate.

So the WLC exists to turn *many dumb radios into one coherent, managed, roamable, secure wireless network*. It's the wireless twin of the SDN idea — **separate the brain (control plane) from the muscle (the APs' radios)** — and, notably, the **same pattern** you'll meet again as a whole‑fabric **SDN controller** (Catalyst Center) in Block 10.

🔵 **Why the hardware depth?** Because *where the WLC sits and how it switches* determines whether it's a graceful conductor or a traffic jam. A WLC in **central switching** is in the **data path of every wireless byte** — misjudge its throughput or uplink and the whole WLAN throttles. Understanding that is the difference between "add APs" and "the WLC is the bottleneck."

## 5 · Description 📋

- **Wireless LAN Controller (WLC)** 🟢 — the device that manages **lightweight APs** and centralizes their control (auth, RF, roaming, policy). Connects to the wired network typically via a **LAG**.
- **Deployment models** 🔵:
  - **Centralized appliance** — a physical 9800 (‑80/‑40/‑L) in the data center; APs tunnel back over CAPWAP.
  - **Cloud (virtual)** — **9800‑CL** as a VM on‑prem/private cloud; scale chosen by template.
  - **Embedded on a switch** — the 9800 controller software runs *inside* a Catalyst 9000 switch for smaller sites.
  - **EWC (Embedded Wireless Controller on AP)** — one capable AP hosts the controller function for a small site.
  - **Cloud‑managed (Meraki)** — no on‑prem controller; APs are managed from the cloud dashboard.
- **Switching modes** 🟢🔵:
  - **Central switching (local mode)** — client traffic tunnels to the WLC, which bridges it to the wired VLAN.
  - **FlexConnect (local switching)** — the AP bridges client traffic locally; the WLC does control only. Branch‑friendly (survives WAN loss).
- 🔵 **WLC ≠ SDN controller.** A WLC controls **wireless**; a **network/SDN controller** (Cisco **Catalyst Center**) controls a **whole fabric** of wired+wireless devices. Both split control from data; the SDN controller is the Block‑10 topic.

## 6 · How It Works — under the hood ⚙️

### 6.1 An AP joins its controller 🔵
```
1. AP boots, gets an IP (DHCP) on its mgmt VLAN
2. Discovers WLCs: DHCP option 43 / DNS (CISCO-CAPWAP-CONTROLLER) / remembered / broadcast
3. Sends CAPWAP join request to the best WLC
4. WLC authenticates the AP by X.509 certificate (blocks rogues), pushes config + image
5. AP is now a managed, thin radio — control tunnel (5246/DTLS) stays up for life
```
### 6.2 What the WLC does continuously (split‑MAC "WLC side")
- **RRM** — watches all APs' RF reports, assigns **channels & transmit power** to minimize interference network‑wide.
- **Authentication** — brokers 802.1X/RADIUS, PSK, guest.
- **Roaming/mobility** — keeps each client's state so AP→AP handoff is seamless; coordinates roaming across WLCs via **mobility groups**.
- **Security** — **aWIPS** rogue/attack detection; policy/QoS enforcement.

### 6.3 The data path — and why the mode matters 🔑
```
CENTRAL SWITCHING (local mode):
  client → AP → CAPWAP data tunnel → WLC → de-encapsulate → wired VLAN
  ⇒ EVERY client byte crosses the WLC ⇒ WLC throughput & uplink are the ceiling

FLEXCONNECT (local switching):
  client → AP → (bridged locally onto the wired VLAN at the AP)
  ⇒ client data NEVER touches the WLC ⇒ WLC only carries lightweight control
```
### 6.4 SSO failover 🔵
Two WLCs form an **active/standby pair** over the **redundancy port (RP)**. The standby **mirrors live state** (APs, clients, sessions). If the active fails, the standby takes over **statefully** — clients and APs stay up, no re‑join, no re‑auth. That's **1:1 SSO**; **N+1** shares one backup across several active WLCs more economically.

## 7 · Pros & Cons ⚖️

| Aspect | ✅ Strength | ⚠️ Trade‑off |
|---|---|---|
| **Central control (RRM, roaming)** | One brain; coordinated RF; seamless roaming; scale | The brain is a dependency/failure domain |
| **Central switching** | Full visibility & policy on all client traffic | WLC becomes a **data‑path bottleneck** (§9.4 ①) |
| **FlexConnect** | Survives WAN loss; offloads WLC data path | Less central visibility; more AP‑side config |
| **Appliance (9800‑80)** | Highest scale, 100GE, hot‑swap modules | Cost; rack space; another box to power/cool |
| **Virtual (9800‑CL)** | Runs on your hypervisor; flexible scale | Bounded by host resources (§9.4 ⑤) |
| **EWC / embedded / cloud** | No dedicated controller box | Lower scale ceilings |
| **SSO 1:1** | Instant stateful failover | A whole standby WLC sits idle (capacity cost) |

## 8 · Variants — the tier descent 🪜

> Flagship appliance described in full above. Each rung trades **scale, uplink speed, and dedicated hardware**.

| Tier | Example | vs the 9800‑80 — what changes | Ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flagship appliance** 🔵 | **Catalyst 9800‑80** | *baseline* — hot‑swap **10/40/100 GE** modules, dual PSU, SSO | **6000 APs / 64,000 clients** |
| **Mid appliance** | **Catalyst 9800‑40** | Fewer/slower ports; no 100GE modularity | ~2000 APs / 32,000 clients |
| **Small appliance** | **Catalyst 9800‑L** | **2.5G/1G** data ports; copper *or* fiber; ~**5 Gbps** | **250 → 500 APs** (license), 5–10k clients |
| **Virtual (cloud)** | **Catalyst 9800‑CL** | No hardware of its own; **scale by template** on a hypervisor | Small 1000 / … / Large 6000 APs |
| **Embedded on switch** | 9800 on Catalyst 9300/9400 | Controller software runs **inside a switch** | ~200 APs (site‑sized) |
| **EWC on AP** | Embedded Wireless Controller | **An AP hosts the controller** for a small site | ~100 APs |
| **Cloud‑managed** | Meraki dashboard | **No on‑prem controller** — cloud is the brain | Bounded by dashboard/licensing |

**Through‑line:** descending, you lose **scale → uplink speed → dedicated hardware → then the on‑prem controller itself** (EWC folds it into an AP; Meraki moves it to the cloud). The *management function* survives to the bottom; the *dedicated, high‑throughput data‑plane box* disappears first.

### 9.2 · Class‑specific expansions (the WLC "add‑module" family)

- **Hot‑swap uplink modules** 🔵 — 10/40/100 GE QSFP on the 9800‑80; scale the wired pipe without replacing the box.
- **Licensing as a "capacity module"** 🔑 — the **Performance license** lifts the 9800‑L from **250 → 500 APs**; per‑AP **DNA/Networking subscriptions** entitle APs to join. *Scale is bought, not just built.*
- **Form‑factor as deployment choice** — appliance / **9800‑CL VM** / **embedded‑on‑switch** / **EWC‑on‑AP** / cloud.
- **Redundancy pairing** — 1:1 SSO pair, or **N+1** shared standby; **mobility groups** to extend roaming across multiple WLCs.
- **SMU hot/cold patching** 🔵 — install bug/security fixes without a full image swap or network‑wide downtime.

### 9.4 · ⚡ Relationship callouts — which spec limits which, and what breaks

> Same lesson: the **shared resource upstream** sets the real limit.

**① Throughput ↔ switching mode — the WLC archetype.** 🔑
A WLC's throughput number (e.g. **5 Gbps** on the 9800‑L) is the ceiling **only in central switching**, where *every* client byte tunnels through it. Switch to **FlexConnect** and client data bridges at the AP — the same WLC now supports far more real traffic because it carries **control only**. *The brain is a bottleneck only when you push data through it.* This is the wireless echo of "the shared resource upstream limits the headline number."

**② Max scale ↔ licensing.** 🔑 The 9800‑L hardware does **500 APs**, but only **250** without the **Performance license**. And every AP needs its own **subscription** to join at all. *Silicon sets the maximum; licenses set the actual ceiling.* Plan (and budget) both.

**③ Uplink / LAG ↔ aggregated client traffic (central mode).** In central switching, the WLC's uplink must carry the **sum** of all APs' client traffic. If the LAG's bandwidth < aggregate demand, the WLAN throttles at the controller — no matter how fast the APs are. *Size the LAG to the aggregate air behind it.*

**④ SSO redundancy ↔ usable capacity.** 🔵 A **1:1** standby mirrors the active and **does no work until failover** — you buy two WLCs to get one WLC's worth of guaranteed service. **N+1** shares one standby across several actives (cheaper, slightly less instant). *Redundancy is capacity you deliberately hold in reserve* — same logic as N+1 PSUs in `1.1.2`.

**⑤ Virtual scale template ↔ host resources.** 🔵 A **9800‑CL** "Large" (6000 APs) only performs if the hypervisor host gives it the required **vCPU/RAM** (cross‑ref `1.1.2`). Under‑provision the VM and the template's number is fiction. *A virtual WLC is bounded by the physical host beneath it.*

**⑥ Control‑plane CPU ↔ AP/client count & churn.** 🔵 Every AP join, client auth, and **roam** is control‑plane work. Scale tiers exist because the **control CPU** — not the data plane — caps how many APs/clients/roams‑per‑second a WLC can service. A high‑roaming environment (warehouse, stadium) stresses this well before raw client count does.

**⑦ Mobility group size ↔ seamless roaming reach.** 🔵 Clients roam seamlessly only **within** a mobility group; crossing beyond it means re‑auth/IP change. The group's scale (WLCs + APs it spans) defines the seamless‑roaming domain. *Design the mobility domain to the users' movement, not the org chart.*

## 10 · Best Practices ✅

- **Choose the switching mode deliberately:** **central** for full visibility/policy in a campus with a fat WLC uplink; **FlexConnect** for branches (survives WAN loss, offloads the WLC data path) (§9.4 ①).
- **Size the WLC to APs, clients, *and* roam rate** — not just client count. High‑mobility sites need control‑plane headroom (§9.4 ⑥).
- **Deploy SSO (1:1) for campus cores; N+1 where budget matters.** Always give SSO its **dedicated RP** link.
- **Right‑size 9800‑CL VMs** to the chosen scale template's vCPU/RAM, and place them with server best‑practice (NUMA, redundancy) from `1.1.2`.
- **Bundle uplinks into a LAG** across two upstream switches; size it to the **aggregate** client traffic in central mode (§9.4 ③).
- **Track licenses as capacity:** confirm the Performance license / per‑AP subscriptions cover current *and* planned AP counts before you hit the wall (§9.4 ②).
- **Secure & isolate the controller:** it holds every client's keys and can reconfigure the whole WLAN — management VLAN, hardened access, patched via SMUs.

---

## 11 · No‑Goes 🚫

- ❌ **Reading the throughput number as an absolute.** It's a **central‑switching** ceiling; in FlexConnect it barely applies (§9.4 ①). Conversely, don't run heavy central switching through an under‑spec'd WLC.
- ❌ **Assuming hardware scale = usable scale.** Without the Performance license / AP subscriptions you hit the licensed ceiling, not the silicon one (§9.4 ②).
- ❌ **Central switching with a thin WLC uplink.** All client traffic funnels through it; a 1G LAG behind 2000 APs is a traffic jam (§9.4 ③).
- ❌ **Counting a 1:1 standby as extra capacity.** It's reserve — sizing your load to *both* WLCs means no redundancy at all (mirror of the N+1‑PSU trap in `1.1.2`).
- ❌ **Under‑provisioning a 9800‑CL VM** and expecting the "Large" template's numbers (§9.4 ⑤).
- ❌ **Ignoring roam rate.** A stadium with modest client counts but constant roaming can exhaust the control plane a campus never would (§9.4 ⑥).
- ❌ **Stretching seamless roaming beyond the mobility group** and wondering why clients drop/re‑auth at the edges (§9.4 ⑦).
- ❌ **Confusing the WLC with an SDN controller.** A WLC runs wireless; **Catalyst Center** runs the whole fabric (Block 10). Different scope, different box.
