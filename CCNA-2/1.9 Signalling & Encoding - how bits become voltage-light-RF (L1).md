## 3 · TL;DR ⚡

- A **bit (1/0) is abstract**; a wire carries **voltage**, a fiber carries **light**, the air carries **RF**. **Encoding** is the machinery that converts bits ↔ physical signals — and it's more than "high = 1, low = 0", because raw signalling has three problems it must solve.
- 🔑 **Encoding solves three problems at once:** ① **clock recovery** — the receiver has **no separate clock wire**, so it must recover timing from the signal's **transitions**; ② **DC balance** — the medium (via transformers/magnetics, `1.5`) can't pass a DC bias, so the code must keep **equal 1s and 0s** over time; ③ **efficiency** — pack the **most bits per symbol** the medium's noise allows.
- **Line codes** you'll meet: **NRZ** (simple, but long runs break clocking), **Manchester** (10BASE‑T — a guaranteed mid‑bit transition → self‑clocking, but **doubles the baud**), **4B/5B** (100BASE‑TX — 4 bits→5 to guarantee transitions) often paired with **MLT‑3** (3 voltage levels, slow transitions → fits in far less bandwidth), **8B/10B** (25% overhead) and **64B/66B** (~3% overhead — why 10G+ switched to it).
- 🔑 **Baud ≠ bit rate.** Baud = **symbols/sec**; each symbol can carry **multiple bits** if it has **multiple levels** (**bits/symbol = log₂(levels)**). **PAM‑4** = 4 levels = **2 bits/symbol**; **QAM‑256** = **8 bits/symbol**; **QAM‑1024** (Wi‑Fi 6) = **10 bits/symbol**. More bits per symbol = more speed at the same frequency.
- 🔑 **But more levels = less noise margin (Shannon).** Packing more levels into the same voltage/constellation range makes them **closer together** → **more sensitive to noise** → needs a **cleaner channel** (better cable/SNR). That's the fundamental trade: bandwidth × log₂(1+SNR) = capacity.
- **Ethernet's speed evolution is an encoding evolution:** Manchester (10M) → 4B5B+MLT‑3 (100M) → **PAM‑5 on 4 pairs** (1G) → PAM‑16 (10G) → **PAM‑4** (25G–400G) — each step packs **more bits per symbol** and/or uses **more pairs** to go faster on **bandwidth‑limited copper**.
- **Same bits, three carriers:** copper = **voltage**, fiber = **light on/off**, air = **modulated RF (QAM/OFDM)** — encoding/modulation adapts the bits to each medium.

## 4 · Importance — the problem first 🎯

**The problem:** every layer above this one deals in **bits** — clean, abstract 1s and 0s. But **the physical world has no bits.** A copper pair has a **voltage**; a fiber has **photons**; the air has **electromagnetic waves**. Somewhere, something must **turn an abstract 1 into a real, measurable physical thing** at the transmitter, and **turn it back into a 1** at the receiver — reliably, at gigabits per second, over a noisy, lossy medium. That conversion is **signalling and encoding**, and the naive approach ("send 5 V for a 1, 0 V for a 0") **fails** for three concrete reasons:

1. **The receiver has no clock.** To read the signal, the receiver must sample it at exactly the right instants — but there's **no separate wire carrying the clock**. It has to **recover timing from the signal itself.** If you send a long run of identical bits (00000000), the signal is **flat** — no transitions — and the receiver's clock **drifts** until it loses count. → encoding must **guarantee regular transitions** (Manchester always transitions; 4B/5B limits how many identical bits can occur in a row).
2. **The medium can't pass DC.** Copper Ethernet is **transformer‑coupled** (the magnetics from `1.5`, also how PoE rides the wire, `1.2`) — and transformers **block DC**. If your signal has more 1s than 0s over time, it develops a **DC bias** the transformer strips away, corrupting the data. → encoding must maintain **DC balance** (equal average of high and low).
3. **Raw binary wastes the medium.** A two‑level signal carries **one bit per symbol**. But the cable can carry a certain **symbol rate** (set by its bandwidth, `1.5`), so if you want **more speed than that**, you must carry **more bits per symbol** — using **more voltage levels** (PAM) or, on RF, **more constellation points** (QAM). → encoding **packs multiple bits into each symbol**, bounded by how much **noise** the medium has.

So signalling/encoding is the **foundation everything else stands on**: it's the reason a Cat6A cable can carry 10 Gbps, a fiber 400 Gbps, and Wi‑Fi 7 multi‑gigabit — not by sending bits faster and faster, but by **cleverly mapping bits to symbols** that recover their own clock, stay DC‑balanced, and squeeze the maximum bits per symbol out of the channel's noise budget. It's also *why* higher cable categories (`1.5`) and better SNR matter: **more bandwidth or a cleaner channel lets you use more levels → more bits.**

🔵 **Why the depth?** CCNA doesn't test line coding heavily — but this is exactly the "**learn it once, deeply**" foundation that makes the rest click: why Manchester "wastes" bandwidth, why 100BASE‑TX needs only 31 MHz not 125, why 10G needs Cat6A (PAM‑16 packs 16 levels → tiny noise margin → needs a clean cable), why PAM‑4 replaced NRZ at 25G+, and why Wi‑Fi's data rate is a **modulation** (QAM) story. Understand encoding, and the entire physical layer stops being magic.

## 5 · Description 📋

**Encoding vs modulation** 🔵:
- **Line coding / encoding** — mapping bits to a **baseband** signal directly on the medium (voltage levels on copper, light on/off on fiber).
- **Modulation** — impressing bits onto a **carrier wave** (needed for **RF/wireless** and WDM optical): vary the carrier's **amplitude, frequency, or phase**.

**Line codes** 🔵:
- **NRZ (Non‑Return to Zero)** — high = 1, low = 0. Simple; **no guaranteed transitions** (clock drift on long runs) and **no DC balance**.
- **NRZI** — a **transition** means 1, no transition means 0.
- **Manchester** (10BASE‑T) — a **transition in the middle of every bit** (up = 1, down = 0). **Self‑clocking + DC‑balanced**, but **doubles the baud**.
- **4B/5B** (100BASE‑TX/FX) — map every **4 data bits to a 5‑bit code** chosen to limit consecutive identical bits (guarantees transitions).
- **8B/10B** (1000BASE‑X) — **8→10 bits**; clock + DC balance; **25% overhead**.
- **64B/66B** (10GBASE‑R+) — **64→66 bits**; only **~3% overhead** (why high speeds use it).
- **MLT‑3** (100BASE‑TX) — **3 voltage levels** (+1/0/−1), transitions one level at a time → **cuts required bandwidth**.

**Multi‑level signalling** 🔵:
- **PAM (Pulse Amplitude Modulation)** — multiple voltage levels per symbol: **PAM‑4** (4 levels, 2 bits), **PAM‑5** (1000BASE‑T), **PAM‑16** (10GBASE‑T).
- **QAM (Quadrature Amplitude Modulation)** — RF: combine amplitude + phase into a **constellation**: **QAM‑16** (4 bits), **QAM‑256** (8 bits), **QAM‑1024/4096** (Wi‑Fi 6/7).
- **OFDM** — split data across **many subcarriers**, each QAM‑modulated (Wi‑Fi, LTE/5G).

**Baud vs bit rate** 🟢 — **baud** = symbols/sec; **bit rate** = bits/sec = baud × **bits/symbol**; **bits/symbol = log₂(levels)**.

## 6 · How It Works — the mechanics ⚙️

### 6.1 The encoding chain 🔑
```
TX:  bits → [ENCODER] → symbols (with clock + DC-balance built in) → [DRIVER] → physical signal (V/light/RF)
RX:  physical signal → [CLOCK RECOVERY] extract timing from transitions → [SAMPLER] read symbols → [DECODER] → bits
```

### 6.2 Clock recovery — why transitions matter 🔑
```
There is NO separate clock wire. The receiver runs a PLL that LOCKS to the signal's TRANSITIONS.
Long run of identical bits → no transitions → PLL DRIFTS → bit errors.
→ codes GUARANTEE transitions:  Manchester (every bit) · 4B5B/8B10B (limit run length) · scramblers (10GBASE-T)
```

### 6.3 DC balance — why equal 1s and 0s 🔵
```
Copper Ethernet is TRANSFORMER-coupled (magnetics, 1.5) → transformers BLOCK DC.
A code with more 1s than 0s builds a DC bias the transformer strips → corruption.
→ codes keep the running average balanced: Manchester (inherently), 8B10B (bounded disparity).
(This is the same magnetics that let PoE ride the cable as common-mode DC — 1.2.)
```

### 6.4 Ethernet's encoding evolution (bits per symbol × pairs) 🔑
```
10BASE-T   : Manchester, 2-level, 1 pair       → self-clocking but 20 Mbaud for 10 Mbps (Cat3 OK)
100BASE-TX : 4B/5B then MLT-3, 3-level, 1 pair → 125 Mbaud, MLT-3 folds it into ~31.25 MHz (Cat5)
1000BASE-T : 4D-TCM + PAM-5, 5-level, ALL 4 pairs BIDIRECTIONAL (echo cancellation)
             → 125 Mbaud/pair × 2 bits × 4 pairs = 1 Gbps (Cat5e)
10GBASE-T  : PAM-16 (DSQ128), 16-level, 4 pairs → 800 Mbaud/pair (needs Cat6A — tiny noise margin)
25G–400G   : PAM-4, 4-level, 2 bits/symbol      → the modern high-speed workhorse
FIBER      : 1000BASE-X = 8B/10B (25% overhead) · 10GBASE-R = 64B/66B (~3%) — light on/off
```
*Each step packs **more bits per symbol** (more levels) and/or uses **more pairs** — because raw copper bandwidth is limited (`1.5`), you can't just "clock faster."*

### 6.5 Modulation on RF (previewing `1.11`) 🔵
```
Wireless can't send baseband — it modulates a CARRIER:
  ASK (amplitude) · FSK (frequency) · PSK (phase) · QAM (amplitude+phase constellation)
  QAM-256 = 8 bits/symbol · QAM-1024 (Wi-Fi 6) = 10 · QAM-4096 (Wi-Fi 7) = 12
  OFDM: split across many subcarriers, each QAM-modulated → robust, high-rate (Wi-Fi, 5G)
```

### 6.6 The limits: Nyquist & Shannon 🔵
```
NYQUIST (noiseless):  max bit rate = 2 × Bandwidth × log2(Levels)   → more bandwidth OR more levels = more bits
SHANNON (with noise): Capacity     = Bandwidth × log2(1 + SNR)      → NOISE caps how many levels you can use
```
*This is why higher Cat cables (more MHz, `1.5`) **and** cleaner channels (higher SNR) both raise speed — and why PAM‑16 (10G) demands Cat6A.*

### 6.7 ⚡ Signalling relationships (the "why") 🔑
> Encoding is a set of trade‑offs among clocking, DC balance, bits/symbol, and noise.

**① Bits ↔ symbols ↔ signal states — the whole job.** Encoding maps **bits → symbols → physical states** (voltage/light/phase), and the receiver reverses it. *L1 signalling exists to make abstract bits physically transmittable and recoverable.*

**② Self‑clocking ↔ transitions.** No clock wire → the receiver recovers timing from **transitions** → codes must **guarantee** them (Manchester always; 4B5B/8B10B limit run length). *A code that allows long flat runs will lose the clock — transitions are the clock* (§6.2).

**③ DC balance ↔ transformer coupling.** Magnetics (`1.5`) block DC → codes must keep **equal 1s/0s** → Manchester/8B10B are DC‑balanced. *The same coupling that lets PoE share the wire (`1.2`) forces DC‑balanced encoding* (§6.3).

**④ Baud vs bit rate ↔ bits/symbol.** Speed = baud × **log₂(levels)**. More levels (PAM‑4, QAM‑256) = **more bits per symbol** = higher rate **without** raising the symbol rate. *You can go faster by clocking faster **or** by carrying more bits per symbol — encoding chooses the latter when bandwidth is scarce* (§6.4).

**⑤ Levels ↔ noise margin (Shannon) — the master trade.** 🔑 Packing more levels into the same range makes them **closer together** → **less tolerant of noise** → needs a **cleaner channel** (better cable/SNR). *More bits per symbol is bought with noise margin — which is why 10GBASE‑T (PAM‑16) needs Cat6A and Wi‑Fi's top QAM needs a strong, clean signal* (§6.6).

**⑥ Encoding overhead ↔ efficiency.** **8B/10B** spends **25%** of the line rate on coding; **64B/66B** spends **~3%**. *At 10G+ the 25% penalty was unaffordable, so high speeds switched to 64B/66B — the overhead itself became a design driver* (§6.4).

**⑦ Bandwidth ↔ capacity (Nyquist/Shannon).** More **bandwidth** (higher Cat, `1.5`) **or** more **levels** (needs higher SNR) = more capacity. *Cable category and encoding scheme are two knobs on the same capacity equation* (§6.6).

**⑧ Medium ↔ signal type.** Copper = **voltage**, fiber = **light on/off**, air = **modulated RF**. Same bits, three physical carriers; encoding/modulation adapts to each. *The bits don't change — how they're painted onto the medium does* (`1.5`/`1.6`/`1.11`).

**⑨ MLT‑3/pulse‑shaping ↔ bandwidth reduction.** 100BASE‑TX's **MLT‑3** (3 levels, one‑step transitions) folds a 125 Mbaud stream into **~31 MHz** — so Fast Ethernet fits on Cat5. *Clever multi‑level, slow‑transition coding buys speed on modest cable* (§6.4).

**⑩ Encoding evolution ↔ speed demands.** Every Ethernet speed jump is an **encoding jump** (Manchester → 4B5B/MLT‑3 → PAM‑5/4D → PAM‑16 → PAM‑4), plus using **more pairs**. *You don't reach 10G by clocking 10× — you reach it by packing more bits per symbol across more pairs* (§6.4).

## 7 · Pros & Cons ⚖️

| Encoding | ✅ Strength | ⚠️ Trade‑off |
|---|---|---|
| **NRZ** | Simplest; 1 bit/symbol | No clock/DC guarantees; long runs fail |
| **Manchester** | Self‑clocking, DC‑balanced | **Doubles baud** (bandwidth‑hungry) |
| **4B/5B + MLT‑3** | Guarantees transitions; fits Cat5 | Coding overhead; 3‑level complexity |
| **8B/10B** | Robust clock + DC balance | **25% overhead** |
| **64B/66B** | ~3% overhead (efficient) | Needs good clock recovery (scrambling) |
| **PAM‑4/5/16** | Many bits/symbol → high speed | **Shrinking noise margin** → needs clean channel (§6.7 ⑤) |
| **QAM/OFDM (RF)** | Very high bits/symbol; robust (OFDM) | Top constellations need strong SNR |

## 10 · Best Practices ✅

- **Understand why raw NRZ isn't used** — encoding must guarantee clocking and DC balance, not just represent 1/0 (§6.2‑6.3).
- **Read speed as baud × bits/symbol** — a "faster" link often means **more bits per symbol** (PAM‑4/QAM), not a higher clock (§6.7 ④).
- **Match cable/SNR to the encoding** — PAM‑16 (10GBASE‑T) needs **Cat6A** because more levels = less noise margin (§6.7 ⑤; `1.5`).
- **Expect 64B/66B (not 8B/10B) at 10G+** — the 25% overhead doesn't scale (§6.7 ⑥).
- **Treat Wi‑Fi rate as a modulation story** — higher QAM (256/1024/4096) needs a stronger, cleaner signal (§6.5; `1.11`).
- **Connect it back:** category MHz (`1.5`), SNR, and encoding are all knobs on the **same capacity equation** (§6.7 ⑦).

## 11 · No‑Goes 🚫

- ❌ **Assuming "high = 1, low = 0" is how Ethernet works.** Real codes add clocking + DC balance + multi‑level signalling (§6.1‑6.4).
- ❌ **Confusing baud with bit rate.** They're equal only for 1 bit/symbol; PAM‑4/QAM carry several (§6.7 ④).
- ❌ **Running PAM‑16 (10GBASE‑T) on marginal cable.** Tiny noise margin → errors; needs Cat6A (§6.7 ⑤).
- ❌ **Ignoring DC balance / clock recovery.** Long identical runs on an unbalanced code = drift and corruption (§6.2‑6.3).
- ❌ **Thinking more constellation points is free.** More QAM points = less noise tolerance (§6.5, §6.7 ⑤).
- ❌ **Assuming you can just "clock faster" for more speed.** Bandwidth‑limited copper needs more bits/symbol and more pairs (§6.7 ⑩).
