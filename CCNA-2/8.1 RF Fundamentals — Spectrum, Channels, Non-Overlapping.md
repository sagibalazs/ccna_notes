## 3. ⚡ TL;DR

- **RF = radio frequency**: electromagnetic waves in the ~2.4–7.1 GHz range, described by **frequency, amplitude, phase, wavelength**. Bits ride on these waves by *modulating* them.
- Wi-Fi lives in **3 unlicensed bands**: **2.4 GHz** (long range, only **3** non-overlapping channels: **1, 6, 11**), **5 GHz** (~25 channels, many need **DFS**), and **6 GHz** (Wi-Fi 6E/7 only, 59 channels, pristine).
- **Higher frequency → shorter wavelength → shorter range but more capacity.** This single trade-off explains the whole band lineup.
- Channels are **5 MHz apart but 20 MHz wide** in 2.4 GHz → they smear into each other; only 1/6/11 have clear air between their centers.
- RF is measured in **logarithmic** units: **dB** (relative), **dBm** (absolute power vs 1 mW), **dBi** (antenna gain). Master the **Rule of 3s & 10s** and you can do RF math in your head.
- Waves degrade via **free-space path loss** + **absorption, reflection, refraction, diffraction, scattering** → this is why coverage design is hard.

## 4. 🎯 Importance — The Problem First

**The problem:** You want to network devices *without cables*. The instant you remove the cable, you lose three luxuries wired Ethernet took for granted:

1. **A private medium.** A cable is yours alone. Air is shared with every other transmitter in range — including devices you don't control and can't see.
2. **A collision-free full-duplex link.** On modern switched Ethernet, a port can send and receive simultaneously with zero contention. Radios are **half-duplex** on a channel — a radio can't cleanly hear while it shouts, so everyone must *take turns*.
3. **Predictable, bounded signal.** A signal on copper stays on copper and arrives essentially intact. A radio signal **spreads out, weakens with distance, bounces, and gets absorbed** — its strength at the far end is a physics problem, not a given.

Because the medium is shared, finite, and physically messy, **you cannot just "turn Wi-Fi on."** You have to answer engineering questions:

- *Which slice of spectrum (band) do I use, and what does each cost me in range vs capacity?*
- *How many independent conversations (channels) can physically coexist before they trample each other?*
- *How much signal actually survives the trip from AP to client through walls and air?*

RF fundamentals is the layer that answers all three. Get it wrong and you get the #1 real-world Wi-Fi complaint: **"it's connected but slow / drops."** Nine times out of ten that's not a config bug — it's **co-channel or adjacent-channel interference**, i.e., someone ignored channels 1/6/11, or **weak signal / low SNR**, i.e., someone ignored path loss. You can't troubleshoot what you don't understand physically.

> 🕵️ **Dirty secret:** Wi-Fi "speed" numbers on the box (e.g. "AX3000") are *lab* numbers at point-blank range on a clean channel. In a real office they're fiction. The number that actually predicts user experience is **SNR** (signal-to-noise ratio) — a pure RF quantity from this document — not the marketing megabits.

## 5. 📖 Description

- **Radio Frequency (RF)** is electromagnetic energy oscillating fast enough (roughly 3 kHz – 300 GHz) to radiate off an antenna and travel through space. Wi-Fi uses the **microwave** portion (2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz).
- A radio wave carries information by having one of its properties **modulated** — its amplitude, frequency, or phase is deliberately varied to encode 1s and 0s. (The *modulation schemes* — BPSK, QAM, OFDM — are 🔵 CCNP/CWNA depth; for CCNA you need the four wave *characteristics* below.)
- **Four characteristics of every wave** 🟢:
  - **Frequency (f)** — cycles per second, in **Hertz (Hz)**. 2.4 GHz = 2.4 billion cycles/sec. *Defines the band.*
  - **Amplitude (A)** — the "height"/power of the wave = **signal strength**. Bigger amplitude = stronger signal = travels farther.
  - **Wavelength (λ)** — physical length of one cycle in metres. **λ = c / f.** Higher frequency ⇒ shorter wavelength.
  - **Phase (φ)** — timing offset of a wave vs a reference, in degrees (0–360°). Two identical waves in phase reinforce; 180° out of phase they cancel. (Phase is the whole basis of multipath, MIMO, and beamforming later.)
- **Spectrum** is the total range of frequencies. Regulators (FCC in US, ETSI in EU, MIC in Japan) carve out small **unlicensed** slabs — the **ISM** band (2.4 GHz) and **U-NII** bands (5 & 6 GHz) — where anyone may transmit *without a license* if they obey power and behaviour rules.
- A **channel** is a named sub-slice of a band at a defined **center frequency** and **width** (20/40/80/160/320 MHz). Channels are how multiple non-interfering networks share one band.
- **Non-overlapping channels** are channels whose frequency footprints don't touch — the only channels that can run *next to each other* without corrupting one another.

## 6. 🔧 How It Works — Under the Hood

### 6.1 From frequency to wavelength to range (the master trade-off) 🟢

The one equation that governs everything: **λ = c / f**

| Band | Frequency | Wavelength (λ = c/f) | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.4 GHz | 2.4×10⁹ Hz | **≈ 12.5 cm** | Long waves diffract around & pass through obstacles → **best range & wall penetration** |
| 5 GHz | 5×10⁹ Hz | **≈ 6 cm** | Shorter waves absorbed/blocked more → **shorter range, more channels** |
| 6 GHz | 6×10⁹ Hz | **≈ 5 cm** | Shortest → **shortest range, most/cleanest spectrum** |

> **Why higher frequency = shorter range:** higher-frequency waves carry more energy per cycle and are absorbed more readily by materials (walls, water, human bodies). They also complete more cycles per metre, so they "interact" with obstacles more often. Physically shorter waves diffract (bend) around obstacles less well. Net result: a 6 GHz signal dies faster than a 2.4 GHz signal of equal transmit power.
>
> **Why higher frequency = more capacity:** the higher up the spectrum you go, the more *contiguous unused bandwidth* is available. 2.4 GHz has only ~83 MHz total; 6 GHz has **1200 MHz**. More width = more channels = more simultaneous non-interfering networks = more aggregate throughput.

This is the entire reason all three bands still exist: **you trade range for capacity, and different jobs want different points on that curve.**

### 6.2 Why channels overlap in 2.4 GHz (the 1/6/11 story) 🟢

The 2.4 GHz band defines **14 channels**, but here's the trap:

```
  Channel center frequencies: 2412 + (n−1)×5 MHz   (n = 1…13)
  → channels are spaced 5 MHz apart...
  → but each channel is 20 MHz WIDE (22 MHz for legacy 802.11b DSSS)

  So a 20-MHz-wide channel spills over ±10 MHz from its center,
  stepping on ~4 neighbours on each side.

        ch1        ch6         ch11
   ┌────────┐  ┌────────┐  ┌────────┐     ← 20 MHz each, NO overlap
   │        │  │        │  │        │
  ─┴────────┴──┴────────┴──┴────────┴─   only these 3 fit cleanly
   2412      2437       2462  (MHz)        into the ~83 MHz band

   ch1 ch2 ch3 ch4 ch5 ch6 ...            everything else:
   ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓                  overlapping mush
```

- **Channel centers**: ch1 = **2412 MHz**, ch6 = **2437 MHz**, ch11 = **2462 MHz** — spaced 25 MHz apart, so their 20 MHz footprints have breathing room.
- **Only 1, 6, 11** are non-overlapping in the FCC (channels 1–11) plan. That's it. Three. Forever. This is the single most-tested wireless fact on the CCNA.
- (ETSI allows 1–13, which technically permits a 1/5/9/13 plan in Europe, but **1/6/11 is the universal safe answer**. Channel 14 exists only in Japan for legacy 802.11b.)

> 🕵️ **Dirty secret:** "40 MHz on 2.4 GHz" (channel bonding) sounds like free speed, but it eats **two** of your only three non-overlapping channels, guaranteeing overlap in any populated area. Rule of thumb every wireless engineer follows: **2.4 GHz = 20 MHz channels, always.** Save the wide channels for 5/6 GHz.

### 6.3 The two kinds of interference 🟢

| Type | What it is | Effect | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Co-channel interference (CCI)** | Two APs on the **same** channel (e.g. both on ch6) | They politely take turns (CSMA/CA) → *half the airtime each* → slower, but not corrupted | Actually the *lesser evil*; spread load, lower power |
| **Adjacent-channel interference (ACI)** | Two APs on **overlapping** channels (e.g. ch6 & ch8) | Neither can decode the other; it's just **noise** → retransmits, corruption, big throughput loss | **Use only 1/6/11** so channels never overlap |

The whole point of 1/6/11 is to **convert the un-fixable ACI into the manageable CCI.**

### 6.4 The 5 GHz band and DFS 🟢🔵

5 GHz is split by the FCC into four **U-NII** sub-bands, each with its own rules:

| Sub-band | Frequency | 20-MHz channels | DFS? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **U-NII-1** | 5.150–5.250 GHz | 36, 40, 44, 48 | ❌ No | Default go-to; indoor+outdoor |
| **U-NII-2A** | 5.250–5.350 GHz | 52, 56, 60, 64 | ✅ **Yes** | Shares with weather/radar |
| **U-NII-2C** | 5.470–5.725 GHz | 100–144 | ✅ **Yes** | Biggest sub-band, all DFS |
| **U-NII-3** | 5.725–5.850 GHz | 149, 153, 157, 161, 165 | ❌ No | Default go-to; higher power allowed |

- **~25 non-overlapping 20-MHz channels** vs 2.4 GHz's three — this is 5 GHz's superpower.
- **DFS (Dynamic Frequency Selection)** 🟢: on U-NII-2A/2C channels, Wi-Fi is a *guest* sharing spectrum with military/weather **radar**. The AP must **listen for radar pulses and instantly vacate the channel** (moving all clients) if it hears one. This causes real-world "why did my Wi-Fi hiccup?" events.

> 🕵️ **Dirty secret:** Because DFS channels can kick you off unexpectedly (and cheap consumer gear ships with DFS *disabled*), everyone crowds onto the non-DFS channels (36–48, 149–165). So the "clean" 5 GHz band is often congested on 8 channels while ~17 DFS channels sit empty. An engineer who *enables* DFS often finds pristine spectrum.

### 6.5 The 6 GHz band (Wi-Fi 6E / 7) 🔵

- **5.925–7.125 GHz = 1200 MHz of contiguous spectrum** — more than 2.4 + 5 GHz combined, split into **U-NII-5, 6, 7, 8**.
- Yields **59× 20-MHz**, 29× 40, 14× 80, **7× 160-MHz** channels (and 320 MHz on Wi-Fi 7).
- **No legacy devices allowed.** Only 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6E) and newer may transmit here → no old, slow, chatty devices dragging the band down. Clean slate.
- Power/access classes: **LPI** (Low-Power Indoor, no coordination), **SP** (Standard Power, must query an **AFC** database to avoid hitting licensed microwave links), **VLP** (Very Low Power, portable). *No DFS* — 6 GHz uses AFC instead of radar-listening.

### 6.6 RF measurement & math — dB / dBm / dBi and the Rule of 3s & 10s 🟢

RF power spans a *huge* dynamic range (a transmitter is billions of times stronger than what a receiver detects), so we use a **logarithmic** scale.

| Unit | Type | Reference | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **mW** (milliwatt) | Absolute, linear | — | Raw power (rarely used directly) |
| **dBm** | Absolute, log | 1 mW = 0 dBm | Actual signal power. **−67 dBm** = good; **−80 dBm** = weak |
| **dB** | **Relative**, log | ratio of two powers | Gain or loss (a change), *not* an absolute level |
| **dBi** | Relative, log | isotropic radiator | **Antenna gain** vs a theoretical point source |
| **dBd** | Relative, log | half-wave dipole | Antenna gain vs a dipole. **dBi = dBd + 2.15** |

**The Rule of 3s and 10s** — memorize this and you never need a calculator:

```
   +3 dB  → power  ×2  (double)        −3 dB  → power ÷2  (half)
  +10 dB  → power ×10                 −10 dB  → power ÷10

  Combine them:  +13 dB = +10 +3 = ×10 ×2 = ×20
```

**Worked example — EIRP:**
> **EIRP** (Effective Isotropic Radiated Power) = the *actual* power leaving the antenna.
> **EIRP (dBm) = Tx power (dBm) − cable loss (dB) + antenna gain (dBi)**
>
> AP transmits **40 mW**. Convert: 40 mW = 10 + 3 + 3 dBm... easier: 20 dBm = 100 mW, so 40 mW ≈ **16 dBm**. Add a **6 dBi** antenna, lose **0 dB** cable:
> EIRP = 16 − 0 + 6 = **22 dBm ≈ 160 mW.** (The 6 dB gain quadrupled 40 mW → ~160 mW. ✔ +3 doubles, +3 doubles again.)

**SNR — the number that predicts real performance** 🟢:
```
   SNR (dB) = Signal (dBm) − Noise floor (dBm)

   e.g. Signal −55 dBm, noise floor −95 dBm
        SNR = (−55) − (−95) = 40 dB   → excellent

   Rule of thumb:  SNR > 25 dB good   ·   < 20 dB shaky   ·   < 10 dB unusable
```
- **RSSI** = Received Signal Strength Indicator (the client/AP's report of how strong the signal is, roughly a dBm value).
- **Receive sensitivity** = the weakest signal (dBm) a radio can still decode at a given data rate. Faster rates need *stronger* signal.
- **Noise floor** = ambient RF energy floor (~−90 to −98 dBm typically). The lower (more negative), the better.

### 6.7 How a wave degrades on the way — propagation behaviours 🟢

Even before hitting an obstacle, a signal weakens just by spreading out:

- **Free-Space Path Loss (FSPL)** — as the wavefront expands like a balloon, power spreads over an ever-larger sphere, so power *density* falls with distance² (inverse-square law). This happens in a perfect vacuum with nothing in the way — it's unavoidable, and it's *worse at higher frequencies*.

Then the environment attacks it:

| Behaviour | What happens | Everyday cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Absorption** | Material converts RF energy to **heat**; signal exits weaker | Walls, water, **human bodies** (a room full of people attenuates Wi-Fi) |
| **Reflection** | Wave **bounces** off a smooth surface | Metal, glass, concrete → causes **multipath** |
| **Refraction** | Wave **bends** passing through a medium of different density | Glass, water, atmospheric layers |
| **Diffraction** | Wave **bends around** an edge/obstacle, casting an "RF shadow" | Corner of a building, doorway |
| **Scattering** | Wave **shatters** into many weak waves off a rough/tiny-object surface | Dust, foliage, chain-link fence, rain |
| **Multipath** | Reflected copies arrive at slightly different times/phases | Can reinforce **or** cancel (dead spots) — MIMO later *exploits* this |

> 🕵️ **Dirty secret:** Water absorbs 2.4 GHz strongly — it's literally how a microwave oven (2.45 GHz!) cooks food. That's why a fish tank, a wet umbrella stand, or a crowded room noticeably kills Wi-Fi, and why the human body is treated as an "RF obstacle" in coverage design.

## 7. ⚖️ Pros & Cons — Band Trade-offs

### 2.4 GHz
✅ Longest range, best wall penetration · ✅ Universal device support (every Wi-Fi device ever made) · ✅ Cheap
❌ **Only 3 non-overlapping channels** · ❌ Most congested (Wi-Fi + Bluetooth + microwaves + IoT) · ❌ Lowest throughput

### 5 GHz
✅ **~25 non-overlapping channels** · ✅ Wide channels (40/80/160) → high throughput · ✅ Far less legacy congestion
❌ Shorter range, poorer wall penetration · ❌ **DFS complexity** on most channels · ❌ Not on very old devices

### 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E/7)
✅ **1200 MHz, 59 channels**, contiguous & clean · ✅ **No legacy devices** = no drag · ✅ Wide channels galore
❌ Shortest range · ❌ **Only newest clients** support it · ❌ SP outdoor needs AFC coordination

**The universal law:** ⬆️ frequency = ⬆️ capacity / ⬇️ congestion, but ⬇️ range / ⬇️ penetration.

## 9. 🧩 Devices / Media / Protocols

- **Access Point (AP)** — the radio that bridges wireless clients to the wired LAN; transmits on chosen channel(s) & power.
- **Wireless LAN Controller (WLC)** — centrally assigns channels & power to APs (via **RRM** — Radio Resource Management — which automates 1/6/11 planning and DFS). Detailed in 8.3–8.5.
- **Antenna** — converts RF current ↔ radiated waves; **gain** measured in **dBi**. Omnidirectional vs directional (8.5 depth).
- **Client (STA/station)** — laptop, phone, IoT; has its own Tx power & receive sensitivity.
- **Spectrum analyzer / Wi-Fi scanner** — tools that *see* the RF (e.g. Cisco Spectrum Expert, Ekahau, NetSpot, `wifi` scanners) to spot interference and non-Wi-Fi noise.
- **The medium:** unlicensed **ISM** (2.4 GHz) and **U-NII** (5/6 GHz) spectrum — air, governed by **FCC** (US), **ETSI** (EU), **MIC** (Japan).
- **Related mechanisms:** **CSMA/CA** (how radios take turns — the half-duplex "listen before talk"), **DFS** (radar avoidance), **AFC** (6 GHz coordination).

## 10. ✅ Best Practices

- **2.4 GHz → always 20 MHz, channels 1/6/11 only.** No exceptions in any populated area.
- **Prefer 5/6 GHz** for anything performance-sensitive; treat 2.4 GHz as the legacy/IoT/range band.
- **Plan a 1/6/11 honeycomb** so no two adjacent APs share or overlap a channel:
  ```
     6   1   6   1        adjacent cells never repeat a channel;
     1  11   1  11        same channel is always ≥2 cells away
     6   1   6   1
  ```
- **Turn power *down*, not up.** Overlapping high-power APs cause more CCI. Design for controlled cell size, letting clients roam to the nearest AP.
- **Enable DFS channels** to escape the congested non-DFS 8, *if* your clients support them.
- **Design to SNR, not raw dBm.** Target ≈ **−67 dBm** signal and **> 25 dB SNR** at the cell edge for reliable high data rates.
- **Match channel width to band:** 20 MHz on 2.4, 20/40 on 5 in dense areas, wide (80/160) only where spectrum is plentiful (6 GHz).

## 11. 🚫 No-Goes & Troubleshooting Traps

- ❌ **Using 2.4 GHz channels other than 1/6/11** ("I'll use ch3, it looks empty") → guarantees adjacent-channel interference for you *and* your neighbours.
- ❌ **40 MHz (or wider) on 2.4 GHz** → consumes 2+ of only 3 non-overlapping channels; net loss.
- ❌ **Cranking Tx power to the max** → bigger cells overlap → more co-channel contention, and clients that hear the AP but are too weak to reply ("one-way" / sticky-client problems).
- ❌ **Confusing dB with dBm** → dB is a *change*, dBm is a *level*. "The antenna is 6 dBm" is nonsense; it's 6 dB**i**.
- ❌ **Reading SNR sign wrong** → both signal and noise are negative dBm; SNR is their *difference* and is positive. −55 − (−95) = **+40 dB**.
- ❌ **Assuming more bars = more speed** → a strong signal with a high noise floor (low SNR) is *slow*. Always check the noise side too.
- ❌ **Ignoring non-Wi-Fi interference** → microwaves, cordless phones, Bluetooth, wireless cameras, motion sensors all sit in 2.4 GHz and won't show up in a Wi-Fi-only scan. Use a **spectrum analyzer**.

**Troubleshooting flow when "Wi-Fi is slow":**
```
  Slow/dropping?
     ├─ Check SNR/RSSI at the client   → low? path loss / range → move AP, add AP, ↑ not power
     ├─ Check channel plan             → overlap? → enforce 1/6/11 (2.4) / spread 5 GHz
     ├─ Check for co-channel APs       → too many on same ch? → re-plan, ↓ power
     └─ Check for non-Wi-Fi noise      → spectrum analyzer → find the microwave/camera
```

| **DFS** | Dynamic Frequency Selection — AP must vacate a radar-shared 5 GHz channel on detection. | [TP-Link DFS](https://www.tp-link.com/us/support/faq/4309/) · `./terms/dfs.md` |
| **AFC** | Automated Frequency Coordination — DB-driven access control for 6 GHz standard-power. | [Litepoint 6E](https://www.litepoint.com/blog/an-introduction-to-wi-fi-6e-spectrum/) · `./terms/afc.md` |
| **dB** | Relative logarithmic ratio of two powers (a *change*). | `./terms/db.md` |
| **dBm** | Absolute power in dB relative to 1 mW (a *level*). | [RF Math](https://wifiwizardofoz.com/understanding-rf-math/) · `./terms/dbm.md` |
| **dBi / dBd** | Antenna gain vs isotropic radiator / vs dipole (dBi = dBd + 2.15). | `./terms/dbi.md` |
| **EIRP** | Effective Isotropic Radiated Power = Tx − cable loss + antenna gain. | `./terms/eirp.md` |
| **Rule of 3s & 10s** | +3 dB=×2, −3 dB=÷2, +10 dB=×10, −10 dB=÷10. | [RF Math](https://wifiwizardofoz.com/understanding-rf-math/) · `./terms/rule-3-10.md` |
| **RSSI** | Received Signal Strength Indicator; radio's report of signal level. | `./terms/rssi.md` |
| **Receive sensitivity** | Weakest decodable signal (dBm) at a given data rate. | `./terms/rx-sensitivity.md` |
| **Noise floor** | Ambient RF energy level (~−90 to −98 dBm); lower is better. | `./terms/noise-floor.md` |
| **SNR** | Signal-to-Noise Ratio = Signal(dBm) − Noise(dBm); predicts real speed. | `./terms/snr.md` |
| **Free-space path loss (FSPL)** | Natural weakening as the wavefront spreads (inverse-square). | `./terms/fspl.md` |
| **Absorption / Reflection / Refraction / Diffraction / Scattering** | The five ways matter degrades an RF wave. | `./terms/propagation.md` |
| **Multipath** | Reflected copies arriving out of phase; can reinforce or cancel. | `./terms/multipath.md` |

## 13. 🛠️ Practical Tasks (expanded later in context)

1. **Wi-Fi scan (any OS):** run a scanner (`nmcli dev wifi` / macOS Wireless Diagnostics / NetSpot / WiFi Analyzer app). List every 2.4 GHz network's channel — count how many *violate* 1/6/11. Note the RSSI of each.
2. **λ calculation drill:** compute wavelength for 2.412, 5.180, 6.115 GHz by hand using λ = c/f. Confirm ≈ 12.4, 5.8, 4.9 cm.
3. **RF math without a calculator:** given a 20 dBm AP, a 3 dB cable loss, and a 9 dBi antenna, compute EIRP in dBm *and* mW using only the Rule of 3s & 10s. (Answer: 26 dBm = 400 mW.)
4. **SNR reading:** in your scanner, find your own signal (dBm) and estimate the noise floor; compute SNR. Then walk to the far corner of the building and re-measure — watch path loss and SNR drop in real time.
5. **Packet Tracer 8:** place a Wireless Router / AP and 2 laptops; open the AP's GUI and change the 2.4 GHz channel from Auto to 1, then to a non-standard channel; observe association. (PT abstracts RF, so use it to drill *config location*, not physics.)
6. **Interference hunt:** turn on a microwave oven near a 2.4 GHz laptop mid-scan; watch the noise floor on ch6–11 spike. This *is* the 2.45 GHz water-absorption dirty secret, live.
