## 3. ⚡ TL;DR

- **Three architecture families:** **Autonomous** (each AP is a self-contained "fat" AP), **Cloud** (Cisco **Meraki** — dashboard manages, data stays local), and **Controller-based / Split-MAC** (lightweight APs + a **WLC**, bound by **CAPWAP**).
- **CAPWAP** (Control And Provisioning of Wireless Access Points, **RFC 5415**, successor to Cisco's **LWAPP**) tunnels AP↔WLC over **UDP 5246 (control)** and **5247 (data)**, secured by **DTLS** — *on by default for control, optional (licensed) for data*.
- **Split-MAC** divides the 802.11 MAC: **real-time** functions stay on the AP (beacons, probes, ACKs, RTS/CTS, retransmit, queuing, over-air crypto); **non-real-time** functions move to the WLC (authentication, association/roaming, key management, RRM, QoS, wired bridging).
- **FlexConnect** = branch survival: control still goes to a central WLC, but client **data is switched locally**, and the AP keeps working (**standalone mode**) if the WAN/WLC dies.
- **Platforms:** legacy **AireOS** WLCs → current **Catalyst 9800** (IOS-XE), which also comes **virtual (9800-CL, cloud)** and **embedded** (on a switch, or on an AP = **Mobility Express / EWC**).
- The payoff: **centralized RRM, roaming, security, and single-pane config** for hundreds of APs instead of per-AP hand-tuning.

## 4. 🎯 Importance — The Problem First

**The problem:** Everything in 8.1–8.2 works beautifully for *one* AP. Now deploy an enterprise: **hundreds of APs**, five floors, thousands of roaming clients. Suddenly the single-AP model collapses under its own weight:

- **Channel planning** (8.1) — with 300 APs you must assign channels/power so neighbours never overlap, and *re-plan* every time an AP fails or a microwave appears. By hand? Impossible.
- **One seamless network** (8.2) — every AP must present the *same* SSID with consistent security, VLANs, and QoS. Configure 300 APs identically by hand and *keep* them identical forever? Error-prone nightmare.
- **Roaming** — a client walking the hallway must hand off AP→AP in milliseconds without dropping its session or re-authenticating from scratch. That requires a *coordinator* that knows the client across all APs.
- **Security & policy** — one rogue-AP detection sweep, one place to push a new password policy, one consistent firewall/QoS treatment. Scattered across 300 independent boxes? Unmanageable.
- **Visibility** — "why is Wi-Fi slow on floor 3?" needs a single view of all APs, clients, channels, and interference. Not 300 separate CLIs.

The **autonomous** model — each AP its own brain — was the original approach and it *does not scale*. The industry's fix was to **separate the brain from the radio**: pull the slow, coordination-heavy "thinking" into a central controller (or cloud), and leave each AP as a fast, dumb-ish **radio head**. That separation is the single most important idea in enterprise wireless, and it's exactly what **split-MAC/CAPWAP** implements.

Why an engineer must own this:
- **It's the shape of every enterprise WLAN you'll ever touch.** "Where do the APs join? What WLC? AireOS or 9800? FlexConnect at branches?" — these are the first questions on any wireless job.
- **Troubleshooting AP joins** (CAPWAP discovery, DTLS, UDP 5246/5247 through firewalls) is a top-5 real-world wireless problem.
- **Design trade-offs are real money:** central vs local data forwarding, controller redundancy, WAN survivability — all flow from understanding these architectures.

> 🕵️ **Dirty secret:** People say a lightweight AP is "dumb" — it isn't. It still does all the **hard-real-time** RF work (ACKing a frame within microseconds, generating beacons on schedule, RTS/CTS timing). What it *gives up* is the **slow** decision-making (who's allowed on, which channel to use, where to bridge traffic). Split-MAC isn't "smart controller, dumb AP" — it's **"slow brain centralized, fast reflexes local."** Physics forces the split: you literally cannot round-trip an 802.11 ACK to a controller across the campus and back in time.

## 5. 📖 Description

**The three architecture families** 🟢:

- **Autonomous (standalone / "fat" / "thick") AP** — a fully self-contained AP that performs *all* 802.11 MAC + management functions on its own. Connects to the switch via an **802.1Q trunk** (to carry multiple SSID→VLAN mappings). Configured and managed **individually**. Optional central *management* (not control) via Cisco **Catalyst Center / Prime Infrastructure**, but each AP still runs autonomously.

- **Cloud-based (Cisco Meraki)** — the *management plane* is lifted out of the building into a **cloud dashboard**. APs are provisioned with near **zero-touch**, monitored and reported centrally over the Internet. Crucially, the **data plane stays local** — Meraki APs forward client traffic straight onto the local LAN; they do **not** tunnel user data to the cloud. The cloud handles config, monitoring, analytics — not packet forwarding.

- **Controller-based / Split-MAC (lightweight AP + WLC)** — the enterprise workhorse. Thin **lightweight APs (LAPs)** pair with a **Wireless LAN Controller (WLC)** over **CAPWAP** tunnels. The WLC centralizes the "thinking"; the AP handles the radio. This is where **CAPWAP** and **split-MAC** live.

**CAPWAP** — *Control And Provisioning of Wireless Access Points* 🟢:
- The protocol that lets one WLC manage many APs and (optionally) tunnel client traffic.
- **RFC 5415**, the IETF-standardized successor to Cisco's proprietary **LWAPP**; adds **DTLS** security.
- Runs at **Layer 3** — both AP and WLC need IP addresses; the tunnel crosses routed networks.
- **Two channels:** **control** = UDP **5246**, **data** = UDP **5247**.
- **DTLS** encrypts the **control** channel **by default**; **data** channel encryption is **optional** and needs a **DTLS license** on the WLC.

**Split-MAC** — the division of 802.11 MAC labor 🟢:
- **Real-time functions → AP** (must happen in microseconds, can't tolerate a controller round-trip).
- **Non-real-time functions → WLC** (can afford the trip; benefit from centralization).

**FlexConnect** (formerly **H-REAP**) 🟢🔵 — a branch/remote mode where the AP keeps its CAPWAP **control** link to a central WLC but switches client **data locally** (no hairpinning across the WAN), and survives WLC/WAN outages in **standalone mode**.

## 6. 🔧 How It Works — Under the Hood

### 6.1 The split-MAC division — who does what 🟢

```
 ┌──────────────── 802.11 MAC, SPLIT ────────────────┐
 │  REAL-TIME  →  ACCESS POINT   (microsecond timing) │
 │   • Beacon generation & transmission               │
 │   • Probe request handling / probe responses       │
 │   • Control frames: RTS / CTS / ACK                │
 │   • Frame acknowledgement & retransmission          │
 │   • Frame queuing & prioritization (airtime)        │
 │   • MAC-layer data ENCRYPTION / DECRYPTION (over air)│
 │   • Buffering & transmitting frames                 │
 ├────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │  NON-REAL-TIME → WLC          (can be centralized)  │
 │   • Authentication & de-authentication              │
 │   • Association / reassociation (mobility/roaming)  │
 │   • Encryption KEY management                       │
 │   • Bridging/translation wireless ↔ wired (802.11→802.3)│
 │   • Fragmentation                                   │
 │   • RRM: channel & power assignment                 │
 │   • QoS / policy enforcement, security management   │
 └────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Why this exact line?** An 802.11 receiver must **ACK a frame within a SIFS** (~10–16 µs). You physically cannot send that decision to a controller 50 km away and back in time — so **ACK/RTS/CTS/beacons stay on the AP**. But deciding *whether a client is allowed on the network*, or *which channel every AP should use*, takes milliseconds-to-seconds and benefits enormously from a global view — so those **centralize on the WLC**.

> 🕵️ **Dirty secret:** Notice **over-the-air encryption/decryption stays on the AP**, but **key management is on the WLC**. The WLC decides the keys (from the WPA handshake) and hands them to the AP; the AP does the actual per-frame crypto at line rate. This is why a WLC can enforce security policy centrally while each AP still encrypts locally — and why in *central* forwarding, client data rides an (optionally DTLS-wrapped) CAPWAP tunnel to the WLC before hitting the wired LAN.

### 6.2 CAPWAP tunnels & DTLS 🟢

```
   AP ═══════════ CAPWAP ═══════════ WLC
      │  CONTROL  UDP 5246  🔒 DTLS ON by default   │
      │  DATA     UDP 5247  🔓 DTLS OPTIONAL (license)│
   Layer 3: both ends have IP; tunnel crosses routed network
   IPv4 → IP protocol 17 (UDP)   ·   IPv6 → IP protocol 136
```
- **Control channel (5246):** config messages, image downloads, client key exchange, keepalives. Each control message must be **acknowledged** before the next is sent (reliable). **Encrypted by default.**
- **Data channel (5247):** encapsulates/tunnels the client's 802.11 traffic to the WLC (in central-forwarding mode). **Encryption optional.**
- **DTLS** = Datagram TLS (TLS for UDP). Protects control (and optionally data) from eavesdropping / MITM.

> 🕵️ **Firewall gotcha:** APs open CAPWAP from a **random source port** toward the WLC's **5246/5247**; the WLC replies from that same port. This **asymmetry** trips up stateful firewall/NAT rules constantly — a classic "AP won't join across the firewall" root cause. Rule of thumb: allow **UDP 5246 & 5247** end-to-end.

### 6.3 How an AP finds & joins its WLC (CAPWAP state machine) 🟢🔵

```
  1. AP boots → gets IP (DHCP)
  2. DISCOVERY — AP finds candidate WLCs via:
        • DHCP Option 43  (WLC IPv4 addr)  / Option 52 (IPv6)
        • DNS: CISCO-CAPWAP-CONTROLLER.localdomain
        • Layer-2/3 broadcast on the subnet
        • Primed / previously-stored WLC list
     → CAPWAP Discovery Request  ──►  ◄── Discovery Response
  3. DTLS session established (control channel secured)
  4. JOIN — Join Request ──►  ◄── Join Response
  5. If AP image ≠ WLC image → download firmware, reboot
  6. Config download → AP enters RUN state (serving clients)
```

### 6.4 Central vs local data forwarding 🟢🔵

The **control** plane is always CAPWAP-to-WLC. The **data** plane is a *design choice*:
- **Central (local mode):** client data is tunneled (CAPWAP 5247) back to the WLC, which bridges it onto the wired VLAN. Consistent policy, but all traffic hairpins through the WLC.
- **Local (FlexConnect):** the AP drops client data straight onto the local switchport/VLAN. No hairpin — essential for **branches** over a WAN.

### 6.5 FlexConnect — branch survivability 🟢

```
  Branch office ──WAN── Central WLC
     [FlexConnect AP]         │ (control only)
        │ client data switched LOCALLY (no WAN hairpin)
     ┌──┴── local switch ──► local LAN / DHCP / internet

  Modes:
   • CONNECTED   — WLC reachable; WLC does its normal CAPWAP functions
   • STANDALONE  — WLC/WAN DOWN; AP keeps switching traffic locally AND
                    authenticates clients locally → Wi-Fi survives outage
```
FlexConnect solves the branch problem: you don't want a $30k WLC in every small office, *and* you don't want branch client traffic crossing the WAN to a central WLC and back just to reach the printer next to it.

### 6.6 Deployment models & platforms 🔵

**Controller placement / form factor:**

| Model | What it is | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Unified / centralized** | Physical WLC appliance in a central site | Campus/HQ |
| **Cloud (C9800-CL)** | *Virtual* WLC on VMware/KVM or AWS/Azure/GCP | No on-prem HW; IaaS |
| **Embedded on switch (EWC)** | 9800 controller software running *on a Catalyst 9300 switch* | Converged small/mid sites |
| **Embedded on AP / Mobility Express** | One AP runs the controller function for the others | Small offices, no dedicated WLC |
| **Cloud-managed (Meraki)** | Dashboard-managed, local data forwarding | Cloud-first orgs |

**Controller OS/hardware evolution:**
- **AireOS** — legacy WLC OS (e.g., 3504, 5520, 8540).
- **Catalyst 9800** — current, **IOS-XE**-based: **9800-40 / 9800-80 / 9800-L** (appliances), **9800-CL** (cloud/virtual), **EWC** (embedded). Supports **HA SSO** (stateful controller redundancy) and modern security (WPA3, 802.1X, iPSK, OWE).

## 7. ⚖️ Pros & Cons

**Autonomous**
✅ Simple for 1–3 APs · ✅ No controller cost/dependency · ✅ AP fully self-sufficient
❌ **Doesn't scale** — per-AP config · ❌ No centralized RRM/roaming/policy · ❌ Manual channel planning

**Cloud (Meraki)**
✅ Zero-touch, dead-simple management · ✅ Central visibility/analytics from anywhere · ✅ Data stays local (no hairpin)
❌ Ongoing **licensing** cost · ❌ **Internet dependency** for management/config · ❌ Less low-level tuning than 9800

**Controller-based / Split-MAC (WLC + LAP)**
✅ Centralized **RRM, roaming, security, single-pane config** · ✅ Scales to thousands of APs · ✅ FlexConnect for branches
❌ WLC = potential **single point of failure** (mitigate with HA SSO) · ❌ Central forwarding can **hairpin** traffic · ❌ More to design/operate

## 8. 🔀 Variants (Common → Specific)

- **Architecture families:** Autonomous → Cloud (Meraki) → Controller-based/Split-MAC. 🟢
- **CAPWAP predecessors:** **LWAPP** (Cisco proprietary) → **CAPWAP** (RFC 5415, +DTLS). 🔵
- **MAC split modes (IETF):** **Split-MAC** (802.11 functions divided AP/WLC) vs **Local-MAC** (AP handles more MAC locally). 🔵
- **Data forwarding:** Central (local mode, CAPWAP-tunneled) vs Local (FlexConnect). 🟢
- **FlexConnect states:** Connected vs Standalone. 🟢
- **WLC form factors:** appliance (AireOS/9800) → virtual (9800-CL) → embedded-on-switch (EWC) → embedded-on-AP (Mobility Express). 🔵

## 9. 🧩 Devices / Media / Protocols

- **Autonomous AP** — self-contained; trunk to switch.
- **Lightweight AP (LAP)** — thin AP; does real-time RF, tunnels to WLC via CAPWAP.
- **Wireless LAN Controller (WLC)** — centralizes non-real-time MAC, RRM, roaming, security. AireOS or Catalyst 9800.
- **Meraki AP (MR / -MR SKU)** — cloud-managed AP; local data forwarding.
- **CAPWAP** — AP↔WLC tunneling protocol (UDP 5246/5247, DTLS). Successor to **LWAPP**.
- **DTLS** — encrypts CAPWAP (control by default, data optional).
- **Catalyst Center / Prime Infrastructure** — central *management* (not control) for autonomous/other fleets.
- **Discovery helpers:** DHCP **Option 43/52**, DNS **CISCO-CAPWAP-CONTROLLER**.
- **Supporting:** 802.1Q trunks (autonomous SSID→VLAN), HA SSO (WLC redundancy), RRM (channel/power automation from 8.1).

## 10. ✅ Best Practices

- **Match architecture to scale:** 1–3 APs → autonomous/Mobility Express; cloud-first org → Meraki; campus/enterprise → Catalyst 9800 controller-based.
- **Allow UDP 5246 & 5247 end-to-end** through every firewall/NAT between APs and WLC — and remember the source-port asymmetry.
- **Use FlexConnect at branches** so client data switches locally and Wi-Fi survives WAN/WLC outages (standalone mode).
- **Deploy WLC redundancy (HA SSO)** so the controller isn't a single point of failure.
- **Let RRM do channel/power** (it enforces the 1/6/11 honeycomb from 8.1 automatically) instead of static per-AP planning.
- **Keep AP and WLC software aligned** — mismatches force image downloads at join and can block joins.
- **Prefer Catalyst 9800 (IOS-XE)** for new deployments; know AireOS exists for legacy/migration (IRCM bridges the two).

## 11. 🚫 No-Goes & Troubleshooting Traps

- ❌ **Managing 50+ APs as autonomous** — unmanageable; no central RRM/roaming/policy.
- ❌ **Blocking UDP 5246/5247** (or mishandling the source-port asymmetry) — the #1 "AP won't join" cause.
- ❌ **Forgetting CAPWAP is Layer 3** — APs and WLC need IP reachability; a purely L2 assumption fails across subnets.
- ❌ **Assuming data is always encrypted** — CAPWAP **data** DTLS is **off by default** and needs a license; only **control** is encrypted by default.
- ❌ **Central-forwarding a branch over WAN** — hairpinning client traffic across the WAN to the WLC and back kills branch performance; use FlexConnect.
- ❌ **Confusing "lightweight" with "does nothing"** — the AP still owns all real-time RF; blame the WLC for auth/assoc/RRM issues, the AP for RF/airtime issues.
- ❌ **No WLC redundancy** — one controller failure downs the whole WLAN; deploy HA SSO / a secondary WLC.

**Troubleshooting map — "AP won't join the WLC":**
```
  AP has no IP?           → DHCP / switchport / VLAN
  AP has IP, no WLC?      → discovery: DHCP Opt 43 / DNS CISCO-CAPWAP-CONTROLLER / reachability
  Discovery ok, no join?  → DTLS/cert time mismatch, UDP 5246 blocked, WLC AP-count/license limit
  Joins then drops?       → image mismatch (download loop), MTU/fragmentation on CAPWAP path
  Joined, clients fail?   → that's a WLC-side non-real-time issue (auth/assoc/WLAN/VLAN) → 8.6/8.7
```

## 13. 🛠️ Practical Tasks (expanded later in context)

1. **Packet Tracer 8 — WLC lab:** drop a **WLC (e.g., WLC-2504/3504)** and 2–3 **lightweight APs** with a switch and DHCP; watch the APs run CAPWAP discovery → join → RUN. Create one WLAN/SSID and confirm it appears on all APs from one config point.
2. **CAPWAP capture (real gear or CML):** sniff the AP↔WLC link; identify **Discovery Req/Resp**, the **DTLS** handshake, **Join Req/Resp**, and traffic on **UDP 5246/5247**.
3. **Option 43 drill:** configure a DHCP scope with **Option 43** pointing APs to the WLC; verify an AP with no primed controller still finds and joins it.
4. **Split-MAC sorting exercise:** given a list (beacons, authentication, RRM, ACK, association, key management, RTS/CTS, wired bridging), sort each into **AP (real-time)** vs **WLC (non-real-time)** from memory. This is a direct exam pattern.
5. **FlexConnect thought lab:** design a 3-branch WAN topology; decide central vs FlexConnect forwarding per site and justify it. Predict what happens to each branch's Wi-Fi if the WAN drops (standalone mode).
6. **Architecture decision matrix:** for three scenarios (home office, 8-AP small business, 400-AP campus), pick autonomous / Mobility Express / Meraki / 9800 and defend the choice on cost, scale, and survivability.

> ➡️ **Next:** **8.4 · AP Modes.** We've established that a lightweight AP joins a WLC via CAPWAP and hands off the non-real-time work. But that same joined AP can be *told what job to do*: serve clients (**Local**), switch branch traffic (**FlexConnect**), silently watch the air for threats and location (**Monitor**), capture packets (**Sniffer**), hunt rogue APs (**Rogue Detector**), analyze spectrum (**SE-Connect**), or become a wireless backhaul (**Bridge/Mesh/Flex+Bridge**). Same hardware, same CAPWAP join — different **mode**. That's 8.4.
