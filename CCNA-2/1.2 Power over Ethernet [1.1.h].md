## 2 · Visualization — the circuit & the ladder 🗺️

**One cable, two payloads.** DC power rides the same copper as the data, injected/extracted at the **magnetics** (transformer) center‑taps so the two never interfere.

## 3 · TL;DR ⚡

- **PoE carries DC power *and* Ethernet data on one twisted‑pair cable**, so a device on a ceiling or wall needs **no separate power outlet** — one cable does both.
- Two roles: **PSE** (Power Sourcing Equipment — the switch or an injector) *supplies* power; **PD** (Powered Device — AP, phone, camera) *consumes* it.
- 🔑 **It's polite, not brute‑force.** Standard PoE runs a **handshake first**: **detect** the PD (look for its **25 kΩ signature resistor**) → **classify** it (measure current → power class) → **power up** (44–57 V) → **monitor** (keep power only while the PD keeps drawing). *That's why plugging a non‑PoE laptop into a PoE port doesn't fry it — no signature resistor, so the switch never applies high voltage.*
- **The standards ladder:** **802.3af** (PoE, 15.4 W) → **802.3at** (PoE+, 30 W) → **802.3bt** (PoE++, Type 3 = 60 W, Type 4 = 90–100 W). Classes **0–8** map power levels; the **PSE always sends more than the PD receives** (cable heat loss).
- 🔑 **The physics is all relationships:** power = **V × I**, so PoE uses **~48 V** (not 5/12 V) to keep **current low** → less **I²R heat loss** over 100 m of thin copper. **4‑pair (bt)** spreads current across all pairs → **less loss/heat per pair** → why high‑power PoE needs 4 pairs. And ~48–57 V stays **under the 60 V safety threshold** → touch‑safe, no electrician needed.
- ⚠️ **Passive PoE is the dangerous cousin:** it dumps a **fixed voltage with no handshake** — cheap, but it can **destroy** a device that wasn't built for it. Standards PoE detects/classifies first; passive doesn't.
- **PoE budget is finite and shared:** the switch's total PoE watts (from its PSU) are divided across all ports — you can't run every port at max class at once (the switch archetype, `1.1.5` §9.4 ③).

## 4 · Importance — the problem first 🎯

**The problem:** the most useful network endpoints live **where there's no power outlet.** A Wi‑Fi AP belongs on a **ceiling**. A security camera belongs on an **exterior wall** or a **pole**. An IP phone sits on a desk that already has one crowded power strip. A door‑access reader is **inside a doorframe**. Running mains power to each of these means an **electrician, conduit, an outlet, and a wall‑wart** — expensive, slow, ugly, and sometimes impossible (you can't put a 230 V outlet on a lamp post easily or safely).

Meanwhile, each of those devices **already needs a network cable.** So the question becomes: *why run two cables (data + power) when the data cable has spare capacity to carry both?*

PoE is the answer, and it solves several sub‑problems at once:

1. **Power reaches anywhere the network reaches.** No outlet, no electrician — the same Cat cable that brings data brings watts. Install an AP on a ceiling with **one drop**.
2. **It must be safe to touch and safe to mis‑plug.** Mains voltage on an RJ‑45 would be lethal and illegal. → PoE uses **low voltage (≤57 V, under the 60 V safety limit)** and **refuses to power a device that isn't a PD** (the detection handshake).
3. **It must not fry the huge installed base of non‑PoE gear.** A network is full of PCs, printers, and dumb switches. → standards PoE **checks first, powers second**.
4. **Power management must scale.** A closet switch feeds dozens of PDs from a shared supply. → **classification + a power budget + LLDP negotiation** let the switch allocate watts intelligently.
5. **Centralized power = centralized resilience.** Put the switch on a **UPS** and every AP, phone, and camera it feeds rides through a power cut — no per‑device battery backup needed.

Remove PoE and every ceiling AP, wall camera, and desk phone needs its own outlet and adapter — a massive cost and a deployment nightmare. PoE exists because **the network cable was already going there, and it had room to carry the power too.**

🔵 **Why the depth?** Because PoE quietly decides whether your APs run at full spec (`1.1.3`), how many powered ports your switch can actually feed (`1.1.5`), whether your cable overheats, and whether a cheap passive injector destroys a $2,000 AP. The "it just powers the device" view hides all of that.

## 5 · Description 📋

- **PoE** 🟢 — a set of IEEE standards for delivering DC power alongside Ethernet data on the same twisted‑pair cabling.
- **PSE (Power Sourcing Equipment)** 🟢 — supplies the power. Two kinds:
  - **Endspan** — the **switch itself** has PoE (the normal case).
  - **Midspan** — an inline **injector** adds power between a non‑PoE switch and the PD.
- **PD (Powered Device)** 🟢 — consumes the power: AP, IP phone, camera, door reader, LED light, thin client, IoT sensor.
- **Power classes 0–8** 🟢🔵 — the PD declares a class; the PSE budgets that many watts for the port.
- **The PSE→PD gap** 🔵 — the PSE sends **more** than the PD receives; the difference is **resistive heat loss** over up to 100 m of cable.
- **Negotiation** 🔵 — a **Layer‑1 electrical** handshake (detection + classification), optionally refined by **Layer‑2** LLDP‑MED/CDP in **0.1 W** steps.
- **Passive PoE** 🔵 — non‑standard: a fixed voltage always‑on, **no handshake** (cheap, risky).

## 6 · How It Works — the handshake & the physics ⚙️

### 6.1 The four‑step handshake (why it's safe) 🔑
```
1. DETECTION
   PSE applies a LOW probe voltage (~2.7–10 V) and looks for the PD's
   signature: a 19–26.5 kΩ (nominal 25 kΩ) resistor across the power pairs.
     • in range  → valid PD → continue
     • open (no resistor / non-PoE device) → NO power ever applied  ← saves the laptop
     • short → fault → no power

2. CLASSIFICATION
   PSE raises the probe to 15.5–20.5 V and measures the PD's current draw.
   The current maps to a power class (0–4 single-event; 5–8 need multi-event or LLDP).
   PSE now RESERVES that class's watts from its budget.

3. POWER-UP
   PSE applies full operating voltage (44–57 V DC). The PD's DC-DC converter
   takes over and powers the device electronics.

4. MONITORING (Maintain Power Signature, MPS)
   PSE watches that the PD keeps drawing a minimum current. If the PD is
   unplugged or stops drawing → PSE removes power within a defined window
   (so a dead port doesn't sit energized).
```
🟢 **This is the whole safety story:** standards PoE **checks before it powers**. Passive PoE skips all four steps.

### 6.2 How power and data share the wire (phantom power) 🔵
Ethernet data is a **differential** (balanced) signal; PoE power is applied **common‑mode** (the same DC offset on both wires of a pair) via the **center‑tap of the Ethernet magnetics** (the isolation transformer in every port). Because the data is differential and the power is common‑mode, they **occupy the wire without interfering** — the transformer passes the AC data and taps off the DC power. This trick is called **phantom power**.

**Which pairs carry power:**
```
Alternative A (Mode A): DC on the DATA pairs — pins 1/2 and 3/6
Alternative B (Mode B): DC on the SPARE pairs — pins 4/5 and 7/8
                         (spare in 10/100; also data pairs in Gigabit)
4-pair (802.3bt):        DC on ALL four pairs simultaneously
```
For 2‑pair PoE the PSE uses **one** mode (A *or* B, never both at once), but every PD must **accept either** — so any compliant PD works with any compliant PSE.

### 6.3 ⚡ The electrical relationships (the "why" behind every number) 🔑
> PoE is physics, and physics is relationships. These are the ones that explain the standards.

**① Voltage ↔ current ↔ loss — why ~48 V.** Power = **V × I**. To deliver a given wattage, a **higher voltage** means a **lower current**. And cable heat loss is **I²R** — it grows with the *square* of current. So PoE uses **~48–57 V** (not 5 V or 12 V) specifically to keep current low and **minimize resistive heat loss** over up to 100 m of thin copper. *The voltage choice is a loss‑minimization decision.*

**② The 60 V safety ceiling ↔ the voltage cap.** PoE stays **under ~60 V** because that's the **Safety Extra‑Low Voltage (SELV)** threshold — below it, the power is **touch‑safe** and needs **no electrician, conduit, or special wiring**. *The upper voltage limit is a safety law, not an engineering preference* — which is *why* PoE can't just crank voltage arbitrarily to reduce current further.

**③ 2‑pair vs 4‑pair ↔ heat per pair.** Spreading the same total current across **4 pairs (bt)** instead of **2** roughly **halves the current per pair**, and since loss is **I²R**, that's about **4× less heat per pair**. *This is the physical reason high‑power PoE (Type 3/4) requires 4 pairs* — you literally can't push 90 W down 2 pairs without excessive heat.

**④ The PSE→PD gap ↔ cable resistance & length.** The PSE always sends more than the PD receives (e.g. **90 W → 71.3 W**) because current flowing through the cable's resistance **dissipates power as heat** (I²R) over the run. **Longer cable, thinner gauge, or lower voltage** all **widen the gap**. *The "lost" watts became warmth in your cable.*

**⑤ Cable gauge/category ↔ deliverable power & heat.** Thicker conductors (**23 AWG** in Cat6/6A) have **less resistance** than thin ones (**26 AWG**), so they lose less and heat less. That's why **Cat5e is the minimum** but **Cat6/6A is recommended for Type 4 (90–100 W)** and long runs. *The cable is part of the power circuit, not just the data path.*

**⑥ Bundle heating ↔ derating.** Many PoE cables **bundled together** trap each other's heat; the whole bundle's temperature rises, raising resistance and risking insulation limits. High‑density PoE may require **derating** (less power/cable) or **better cable**. *Power density is a thermal problem, not just an electrical one.*

**⑦ PoE budget ↔ ports × class.** (the switch archetype, `1.1.5` §9.4 ③) The switch's **total PoE watts** (from its PSU) are **shared** across all ports. A 48‑port switch wanting 48 × 30 W (1440 W) with a 740 W budget **can't power every port at PoE+** — it powers until the budget's spent, then **denies** the rest (**Autoclass** reclaims reserved‑but‑unused watts). *Per‑port capability is bounded by a shared pool.*

### 6.4 Backward compatibility & Layer‑2 negotiation 🔵
- A **bt (PoE++) switch** correctly **detects, classifies, and powers an old af phone**, supplying only the ~12.95 W it negotiates — the intelligence is in the handshake, so newer PSEs safely feed older PDs.
- **LLDP‑MED / CDP** (Layer 2) can **refine** the Layer‑1 class in **0.1 W** steps: a PD can request *exactly* what it needs (e.g. a Wi‑Fi 6E AP asking for 28 W), and the PSE can allocate dynamically — more precise than the coarse hardware classes.

## 7 · Pros & Cons ⚖️

| Aspect | ✅ Strength | ⚠️ Trade‑off |
|---|---|---|
| **One cable for power + data** | No outlet/electrician; install anywhere the network reaches | Bounded by cable distance (100 m) and gauge |
| **Safe by design** | Detects PDs; low voltage; touch‑safe (SELV) | Standards PoE only — passive isn't safe |
| **Centralized power** | Switch on a UPS backs up every PD; central mgmt | Switch/PSU is now a shared power dependency |
| **Standardized classes** | Interop across vendors; graceful backward compat | Coarse classes; needs LLDP for fine control |
| **4‑pair (bt)** | Up to 90–100 W; less heat per pair | Needs good cable; higher PSU/budget demand |
| **Passive PoE** | Cheap, simple (bundled kits) | **No handshake → can destroy non‑PoE devices** |

## 8 · Variants — the full standards ladder 🪜

**IEEE standards (the backbone):**

| Standard | Name | Type | Classes | PSE max | PD max | Pairs | Year |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **802.3af** | PoE | 1 | 0–3 | 15.4 W | 12.95 W | 2‑pair | 2003 |
| **802.3at** | PoE+ | 2 | 4 | 30 W | 25.5 W | 2‑pair | 2009 |
| **802.3bt** | PoE++ / 4PPoE | 3 | 5–6 | 45 / 60 W | 40 / 51 W | 4‑pair | 2018 |
| **802.3bt** | PoE++ | 4 | 7–8 | 75 / 90–100 W | 62 / 71.3 W | 4‑pair | 2018 |

**Power classes 0–8 (PSE / PD watts):** 0 = 15.4/12.95 (default) · 1 = 4/3.84 · 2 = 7/6.49 · 3 = 15.4/12.95 · 4 = 30/25.5 · 5 = 45/40 · 6 = 60/51 · 7 = 75/62 · 8 = 90–100/71.3.

**Pre‑standard & vendor variants (for completeness — no gaps):**

| Variant | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cisco Inline Power** | Pre‑af Cisco PoE (~6–7 W) | Original IP‑phone power |
| **Cisco UPOE** | 60 W over 4 pairs | Pre‑bt; ≈ Type 3 |
| **Cisco UPOE+** | 90 W | ≈ Type 4 |
| **LTPoE++** | Linear Technology pre‑bt high‑power | Up to ~90 W+ |
| **PoLRE** | Power over Long‑Reach Ethernet | Extends PoE beyond 100 m (single‑pair) |
| **Passive PoE** ⚠️ | Fixed voltage (24 V/48 V), **no handshake** | Cheap; **can fry non‑PoE gear** |

**Single vs dual signature (bt):** 🔵 **Single‑signature** PDs present one signature/class for the whole device; **dual‑signature** PDs present an independent signature per pairset (for independent power management of two loads).

**Delivery helpers (form of the PSE/PD path):**
- **Midspan injector** — adds power to a non‑PoE switch's link.
- **PoE splitter** — at the PD end, separates power and data → outputs a **barrel jack + data** for a **non‑PoE** device.
- **PoE extender** — regenerates power/data to reach **beyond 100 m**.
- **Always‑on / Perpetual PoE** 🔵 — power continues through a switch **software reboot** (phones/APs stay up during an upgrade). **Fast PoE** — power delivered at boot before the OS loads.

## 10 · Best Practices ✅

- **Match the standard to the PD's class** — budget **802.3bt** for Wi‑Fi 6E/7 APs and cameras that need it; don't rely on proprietary modes when a standard fits (from `1.1.3`).
- **Budget PoE at the switch before deploying** — sum every PD's class against the switch's **total PoE budget**; if short, add PSU capacity or a higher‑budget switch — and decide whether a 2nd PSU is for **PoE or redundancy** (`1.1.5` §9.4 ⑨).
- **Use Cat6/6A for high‑power PoE and long runs** to keep resistive loss and heat in check (§6.3 ⑤).
- **Mind bundle heat** in dense PoE runs — separate/loosen bundles or derate (§6.3 ⑥).
- **Prefer LLDP‑MED negotiation** where supported for precise allocation instead of coarse hardware classes (§6.4).
- **Put the switch on a UPS** so all PDs ride through outages — the whole point of centralized power.
- **Use Perpetual/Fast PoE** so phones/APs survive switch reboots and come up quickly.
- **Verify 4‑pair is enabled** for Class 5+ — some bt switches default to 2‑pair and need explicit 4‑pair enable (§6.2).

## 11 · No‑Goes 🚫

- ❌ **Using a passive PoE injector with standards gear.** No handshake → it can **destroy** a device that expected negotiated power. Reusing a vendor's passive injector on another vendor's PD is a classic way to fry hardware.
- ❌ **Assuming the port delivers the class's full watts to the device.** The **PD gets less** than the PSE sends (cable loss) — size to the **PD** number (§6.3 ④).
- ❌ **Planning as if every port can run max class.** The **shared budget** runs out; late ports get denied (§6.3 ⑦) — and APs silently under‑power (`1.1.3`).
- ❌ **Cheap/thin/long cable for high‑power PoE.** More resistance → more loss, more heat, less deliverable power, possible instability (§6.3 ⑤).
- ❌ **Ignoring bundle heating** in dense installs — trapped heat can exceed cable limits (§6.3 ⑥).
- ❌ **Forgetting to enable 4‑pair** for Type 3/4 — Class 5+ won't power on 2‑pair (§6.2).
- ❌ **Cranking voltage to "reduce loss."** PoE is capped under ~60 V by safety law (SELV) — that's why it spreads current across pairs instead (§6.3 ②③).
- ❌ **Trusting a non‑PoE device won't be harmed by a standard PoE port.** It won't (no signature → no power), **but the opposite is true for passive PoE** — know which you have.
