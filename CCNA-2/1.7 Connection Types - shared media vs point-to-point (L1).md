## 3 · TL;DR ⚡

- A **connection type** is about whether devices **share one medium** or each has a **dedicated link** — and that single choice decides **collisions, duplex, and CSMA/CD**.
- **Shared media** (a **hub** or old **coax bus**): every device hangs off **one medium**, so it's **one collision domain**. Two devices transmitting at once **collide** → they must **listen before sending** (**CSMA/CD**) and can only **take turns** → **half‑duplex**. Performance degrades as more devices share it.
- **Point‑to‑point** (a **switch port to one device**): a **dedicated link** between exactly two endpoints. There's **no one to collide with**, so **both ends can send simultaneously** → **full‑duplex**, **no collisions**, and **CSMA/CD is disabled**.
- 🔑 **The whole chain follows from sharing:** *shared medium → collisions possible → CSMA/CD (listen‑before‑send) → half‑duplex.* Point‑to‑point **breaks the chain at the first link**: no sharing → no collisions → no CSMA/CD → full‑duplex.
- 🔑 **The switch killed shared media.** A **hub** puts all ports in **one** collision domain (shared, half‑duplex). A **switch** makes **each port its own collision domain** (**microsegmentation**) with a **dedicated full‑duplex link** → no collisions, double the throughput, wire‑speed. This is *why* switches replaced hubs (`1.1.5`).
- **Both ends must agree on duplex** (via autonegotiation, `1.1.1`). If one side is full and the other half, you get a **duplex mismatch** — the classic "up but slow" fault in **`1.8`**.
- Modern LANs are **entirely point‑to‑point, full‑duplex**; shared/half‑duplex survives only in legacy gear or specific wireless/coax media.

## 4 · Importance — the problem first 🎯

**The problem:** put more than two devices on a network and they face a fundamental question — **who gets to use the wire, and when?** If two devices transmit onto the **same medium at the same time**, their signals **overlap and garble** (a **collision**), and both messages are destroyed. Early Ethernet was exactly this: many devices on **one shared cable** (a coax bus, later a hub). The medium was a **party line** — everyone heard everyone, and only one could speak at a time without chaos.

That created a set of tightly linked needs:

1. **A way to share without constant collisions.** Devices needed a rule for accessing a shared medium politely. → **CSMA/CD**: *listen* to the wire (Carrier Sense), transmit only if it's idle, and if a collision happens anyway (Collision Detection), **back off and retry**.
2. **Acceptance that sharing means taking turns.** On a shared medium, only one device can successfully transmit at a time → **half‑duplex** (send *or* receive, not both). Half the potential capacity is structurally unavailable, plus collision overhead.
3. **A way to make it *not* shared.** The real fix wasn't a better sharing rule — it was **removing the sharing**. Give every device its **own dedicated link** to a **switch**, and there's **no one to collide with**: both ends can transmit at once (**full‑duplex**), collisions vanish, and CSMA/CD becomes unnecessary.

So "connection type" is one of the most consequential ideas in Ethernet's history: it's the difference between a **contended party line** (shared, half‑duplex, collisions, degrading with load) and a **private full‑duplex link** (point‑to‑point, no collisions, wire‑speed). The move from **hubs to switches** — from shared to point‑to‑point — is *the* reason modern LANs are fast and predictable. And understanding it is what makes the **collision counters, half/full‑duplex settings, and duplex‑mismatch faults** in `1.8` actually make sense.

🔵 **Why the depth?** Because the terms **collision domain**, **CSMA/CD**, and **half/full duplex** aren't trivia — they're a **causal chain** that explains *why* switches beat hubs, *why* each switch port is its own collision domain, and *why* a duplex mismatch produces late collisions on one side and CRC errors on the other. Get the chain, and half of L1/L2 troubleshooting becomes obvious.

## 5 · Description 📋

**Shared media** 🟢 — many devices connected to **one medium** (a **hub**, which electrically repeats every signal to all ports; or a legacy **coax bus**). All devices are in **one collision domain**.

**Point‑to‑point** 🟢 — a **dedicated link** between exactly **two** endpoints (typically a **switch port** and one device). Each such link is **its own collision domain**.

**Collision** 🟢 — two devices transmitting onto the **same shared medium** simultaneously; their signals overlap and both frames are corrupted.

**Collision domain** 🟢 — the set of devices that **can collide** with each other (i.e. share a medium). A hub = **one** collision domain for all ports; a switch = **one per port**.

**CSMA/CD** 🟢 — *Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection*: the shared‑media access method — **listen** before sending, transmit if idle, **detect** collisions, **back off** and retry. Needed **only** on shared/half‑duplex media.

**Half‑duplex** 🟢 — send **or** receive, one at a time (shared media). **Full‑duplex** 🟢 — send **and** receive **simultaneously** (point‑to‑point).

## 6 · How It Works — the chain & the evolution ⚙️

### 6.1 CSMA/CD on shared media 🟢
```
1. CARRIER SENSE:   a device listens — is the shared medium idle?
2. MULTIPLE ACCESS: if idle, it transmits (any device may try)
3. COLLISION DETECT:if two transmit at once → signals overlap → COLLISION detected
4. JAM + BACKOFF:   both send a jam signal, then wait a RANDOM back-off time, then retry
→ only one frame succeeds at a time → HALF-DUPLEX; as load rises, collisions rise, throughput falls.
```

### 6.2 Point‑to‑point breaks the chain 🔑
```
A dedicated link has exactly TWO endpoints and a separate send + receive path.
→ there is NO ONE ELSE on the medium to collide with
→ both ends may transmit AT THE SAME TIME (FULL-DUPLEX)
→ collisions are IMPOSSIBLE → CSMA/CD is DISABLED (not needed)
→ full send + full receive simultaneously = double the effective throughput
```

### 6.3 The evolution: hub → switch 🔑
```
HUB (shared): repeats every incoming signal to ALL ports → ALL ports = ONE collision domain
              → half-duplex, collisions, performance drops with device count.
SWITCH (point-to-point): each port is a SEPARATE dedicated link → each port = its OWN collision domain
              → "MICROSEGMENTATION": collision domain shrinks to a single link (of one device)
              → full-duplex, no collisions, wire-speed forwarding (1.1.5).
```
*This is the concrete reason switches replaced hubs — they turned a shared party line into private full‑duplex links.*

### 6.4 ⚡ Connection‑type relationships (the "why") 🔑
> One decision — share or not — cascades into collisions, CSMA/CD, and duplex.

**① Shared medium ↔ collision domain ↔ CSMA/CD ↔ half‑duplex — the master chain.** Sharing one medium means two devices **can** transmit at once → **collisions** → they must **listen‑before‑send and back off (CSMA/CD)** → only one succeeds at a time → **half‑duplex**. *Every consequence flows from the single fact that the medium is shared.*

**② Point‑to‑point ↔ full‑duplex ↔ no CSMA/CD.** A dedicated two‑endpoint link has **no one to collide with** → both can send at once → **full‑duplex** → **CSMA/CD is unnecessary and disabled**. *Removing the sharing removes collisions, duplex limits, and the access protocol all at once.*

**③ Collision‑domain size ↔ performance.** On shared media, **more devices = more collisions = worse throughput** (it degrades non‑linearly under load). **Microsegmentation** (a switch) shrinks each collision domain to **one link**, so contention disappears. *Performance is inversely tied to how many devices share a collision domain* (§6.3).

**④ Half‑ vs full‑duplex ↔ effective throughput.** Half‑duplex wastes roughly **half** the link (send‑or‑receive) **plus** collision overhead; full‑duplex uses **both directions fully** → up to **double** the effective throughput, with no collision loss. *Full‑duplex isn't just "no collisions" — it's twice the usable bandwidth.*

**⑤ Duplex must match ↔ mismatch fault.** Both ends must **agree** on duplex (usually via **autonegotiation**, `1.1.1`). One side full + one side half = a **duplex mismatch** → **late collisions** on the half side, **CRC/runts** on the full side, and terrible throughput on an **up/up** link. *The connection type must be consistent on both ends — the mismatch is the archetype `1.8` fault.*

**⑥ Collision domain ↔ broadcast domain (don't confuse them).** A switch makes **each port its own collision domain** but **still forwards broadcasts to all ports** — so all its ports remain **one broadcast domain** (a **router/VLAN** bounds that, Blocks 2–3). *Switches eliminate collisions but not broadcasts — different problems, different boundaries.*

**⑦ Wireless ↔ shared media returns (CSMA/CA).** Wi‑Fi is **inherently shared** (one radio medium, can't detect its own collisions) → it uses **CSMA/CA** (Collision *Avoidance*) instead of CD. *Shared‑media contention never fully died — it moved to the air* (deep in the wireless block).

## 7 · Pros & Cons ⚖️

| Aspect | **Shared media (hub/bus)** | **Point‑to‑point (switch)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Collisions** | Yes (CSMA/CD) | None |
| **Duplex** | Half (take turns) | Full (simultaneous) |
| **Throughput** | Degrades with load; ~half + overhead | Wire‑speed; up to 2× effective |
| **Collision domain** | One (all devices) | One per port (microsegmented) |
| **Scalability** | Poor (worse as devices grow) | Excellent |
| **Cost/era** | Cheap/legacy | Standard modern |
| **Use today** | Legacy; Wi‑Fi (as CSMA/CA) | Every modern wired LAN |

## 8 · Variants — the spectrum 🌿

- **Shared media:** legacy **coax bus** (thicknet/thinnet) → **hub** (repeater, one collision domain) → **Wi‑Fi** (shared radio, CSMA/CA — modern shared media).
- **Point‑to‑point:** **switch port ↔ device** (the universal modern LAN link); router‑to‑router serial/WAN links; a **crossover** link between two devices directly (`1.5`).
- **Duplex:** **half‑duplex** (shared) → **full‑duplex** (point‑to‑point); set by **autonegotiation** (`1.1.1`).
- **Access methods:** **CSMA/CD** (wired shared) · **CSMA/CA** (wireless shared) · **none needed** (full‑duplex point‑to‑point).

## 10 · Best Practices ✅

- **Use switched, point‑to‑point, full‑duplex links everywhere** — that's the modern default; hubs are obsolete (§6.3).
- **Let autonegotiation set duplex on both ends** (or hard‑code both identically) to avoid a mismatch (§6.4 ⑤; `1.1.1`).
- **Think in collision domains** — one per switch port (microsegmented); if you see collisions on a *full‑duplex* link, something's wrong (§6.4 ①③).
- **Don't confuse collision and broadcast domains** — switches bound collisions, routers/VLANs bound broadcasts (§6.4 ⑥).
- **Expect CSMA/CA on Wi‑Fi** — wireless is still shared media (§6.4 ⑦).
- **Replace any remaining hubs** — they force half‑duplex and collisions on everything attached (§6.3, §6.4 ④).

---

## 11 · No‑Goes 🚫

- ❌ **Deploying hubs (shared media) in a modern LAN** — half‑duplex, collisions, poor scaling; use switches (§6.3).
- ❌ **Mismatching duplex across a link** — full on one end, half on the other → late collisions + CRC + slow (the `1.8` fault) (§6.4 ⑤).
- ❌ **Expecting collisions on a full‑duplex point‑to‑point link** — they can't happen; if counted, it's a misconfig/mismatch (§6.4 ②⑤).
- ❌ **Thinking a switch removes broadcasts** — it removes collisions, not broadcasts (that's a router/VLAN) (§6.4 ⑥).
- ❌ **Assuming Wi‑Fi is collision‑free** — it's shared media using CSMA/CA (§6.4 ⑦).
- ❌ **Covering the physical media (copper/fiber) here** — those are `1.5`/`1.6`.

## 13 · Practical Tasks 🧪

1. **Hub vs switch in Packet Tracer** 🟢 — connect three PCs via a **hub**, then via a **switch**; observe collisions/half‑duplex on the hub vs full‑duplex/no collisions on the switch (§6.3).
2. **Trace CSMA/CD** 🟢 — on paper, walk two devices on a shared medium through carrier‑sense → collision → jam → back‑off → retry (§6.1).
3. **Count the collision domains** 🔑 — for a hub with 8 ports vs an 8‑port switch, state how many collision domains each has and why (§6.3, §6.4 ③).
4. **Duplex mismatch** 🔑 — set one switch port full and its peer half; predict the counters (late collisions on the half side, CRC on the full side) and tie to `1.8` (§6.4 ⑤).
5. **Full‑duplex throughput** 🔵 — explain why a full‑duplex 1 G link can move ~1 G each way while a half‑duplex one can't, and why collisions make half‑duplex worse than "half" under load (§6.4 ④).
6. **Collision vs broadcast domain** 🔵 — for a single switch, state the number of collision domains and broadcast domains, and what changes if you add a router/VLAN (§6.4 ⑥).
