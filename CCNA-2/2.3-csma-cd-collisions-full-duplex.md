## 3️⃣ TL;DR

- 🟢 On **shared, half-duplex** media only one station can talk at a time. **CSMA/CD** is the referee: *sense* the wire, *transmit* if idle, *detect* collisions while sending, then recover.
- 🟢 On collision: the sender blasts a **32-bit jam** so everyone notices, then waits a **random** number of slot times (**backoff**) and retries — up to **16 attempts**, then drops the frame.
- 🟢 **Backoff doubles its range each collision** (truncated binary exponential): range `0…2ⁿ−1` slot times, capped at n=10 (`0…1023`).
- 🟢 A **collision domain** = the set of devices whose signals can collide. **Hubs share** one; **switches split** it (each port = its own) — this is **microsegmentation**.
- 🟢 **Full-duplex** (dedicated switch port, separate TX/RX) means **no contention → collisions are impossible → CSMA/CD is turned off.** This is how every modern LAN runs.
- 🔵 **Late collision** (collision *after* slot time) is never normal — it's almost always a **duplex mismatch** or an over-long segment.
- 🔵 At **1 Gbps half-duplex** (rare), the 64-byte frame is too short to detect collisions across useful distances, so **carrier extension** pads carrier to **512 bytes** and **frame bursting** offsets the waste. **10 Gig and above are full-duplex only — CSMA/CD was removed from the standard.**

## 4️⃣ Importance — The Problem First 🎯

**The underlying problem:** Original Ethernet was **one long coax cable** shared by every station (a bus). Radio-style, if two stations transmit at the same instant, their signals **overlap and corrupt** — a **collision**. With no rule, a busy shared wire would degenerate into permanent garbage. Three sub-problems had to be solved *without* a central controller:

1. ❓ *"Is anyone else talking right now?"* → **Carrier Sense** (listen first).
2. ❓ *"We both started anyway — how do we even notice?"* → **Collision Detection** (listen *while* sending) + the **64-byte minimum / slot time** (from 2.2) that guarantees you're still transmitting when the collision echo returns.
3. ❓ *"We collided — how do we retry without instantly colliding again?"* → **randomized backoff** so the two stations pick different wait times.

**Why an engineer must own this even though modern links are collision-free:**

- 🔧 **Your interface counters speak CSMA/CD.** `collisions`, `late collision`, `runts`, `excessive collisions` are all CSMA/CD artifacts. Seeing them on a *supposedly* full-duplex link is a **diagnosis**, not noise — usually a **duplex mismatch**.
- 🔧 **"Collision domain" is the mental tool** for understanding *why switches made networks fast.* Every switch port you add carves the old shared bandwidth into a private lane. Without this concept, "why is a switch better than a hub?" is just memorized trivia.
- 🔧 **It explains distance and speed limits.** The reason 10/100 half-duplex tops out at a ~2500 m diameter, and why Gigabit half-duplex is basically dead, comes straight from the collision-detection physics here.
- 🔧 **Duplex mismatch is one of the most common real-world faults** — a link that "works but is mysteriously slow." Recognizing the CSMA/CD fingerprint (collisions on one side, FCS errors on the other) fixes it in minutes.

## 5️⃣ Description 📋

- 🟢 **CSMA/CD = Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection**, the MAC-layer access method for **half-duplex** Ethernet.
  - **Carrier Sense:** a station listens; it transmits only if the medium is idle. Ethernet is **1-persistent** — it sends *immediately* when the wire goes free (which itself can cause collisions when two waiting stations pounce together).
  - **Multiple Access:** all stations share one medium and contend for it; there's no master.
  - **Collision Detection:** a station keeps listening *while* transmitting; overlapping signals are detected as an abnormal condition.
- 🟢 **Jam signal:** on detecting a collision, the station transmits a **32-bit jam sequence** to guarantee the collision lasts long enough for **every** station to register it. Then it aborts the frame.
- 🟢 **Truncated Binary Exponential Backoff (TBEB):** after the *n*-th collision, the station waits a **random integer × slot time**, drawn from `0 … 2^min(n,10) − 1`.
  - 1st collision → `0–1` · 2nd → `0–3` · 3rd → `0–7` · … · 10th → `0–1023`, then the range **freezes** (truncated) at `0–1023`.
  - **attemptLimit = 16.** After 16 failed attempts the MAC reports **excessive collisions**, **drops** the frame, and leaves recovery to upper layers (TCP).
- 🟢 **Collision domain:** the group of devices/segments whose transmissions can collide with each other. A **hub** joins all ports into **one** collision domain; a **switch** or **bridge** gives **each port its own** (microsegmentation); a **router** also bounds them.
- 🟢 **Half-duplex vs full-duplex:**
  - **Half-duplex:** send *or* receive, not both; shares one channel; needs CSMA/CD. (Hubs, coax, and any negotiated-half link.)
  - **Full-duplex:** send *and* receive simultaneously over separate paths; point-to-point (device ↔ switch port); **CSMA/CD off**, collisions impossible.
- 🔵 **Late collision:** a collision detected **after** the slot time (>512 bit times into the frame). It's *illegal* — the station jams but does **not** retry. Root cause is almost always a **duplex mismatch** or a segment longer than the standard allows.
- 🔵 **Gigabit half-duplex additions (802.3z):** **carrier extension** (pad carrier to 512 bytes so collisions stay detectable) and **frame bursting** (send several frames back-to-back to amortize the waste). Half-duplex only; **10 GbE and faster dropped half-duplex/CSMA/CD entirely.**

## 6️⃣ How It Works — Under the Hood ⚙️

### A) 🔁 The algorithm, step by step (half-duplex)

```
1. Frame ready.
2. CARRIER SENSE: medium busy? → wait (defer) until idle + IFG (96 bit times).
3. TRANSMIT the frame AND keep listening.
4. Collision within one slot time (512 bit times)?
      NO  → keep going → frame completes → ✅ success, reset attempt counter.
      YES → go to 5.
5. Send 32-bit JAM (make sure everyone saw it), abort frame.
6. attempts++.  If attempts == 16 → ❌ drop, "excessive collisions", tell upper layer.
7. BACKOFF: r = random integer in [0, 2^min(attempts,10) − 1];  wait r × slotTime.
8. Go to 2.
```

### B) 🎲 Why *randomized* backoff (and why it doubles)

```
Two stations collide. If both waited a FIXED time, they'd retry together → collide AGAIN, forever.
Random wait → they usually pick different slots → one wins the wire.

Doubling the range as collisions pile up = adapting to load:
  light load → tiny random range → fast retry
  heavy load → wide random range → spreads stations out → fewer repeat collisions

  attempt 1: pick from {0,1}          (2 slots)
  attempt 2: pick from {0,1,2,3}      (4 slots)
  attempt 3: pick from {0..7}         (8 slots)
   ...
  attempt 10: pick from {0..1023}     (1024 slots) ← range frozen here ("truncated")
  attempt 16: give up → drop frame
```

### C) 📐 Slot time → distance (the physics link back to 2.2)

```
Slot time = 512 bit times.
  @10 Mbps : 512 bits = 51.2 µs  ⟶ light travels ~ round trip of a ~2500 m network
  @100 Mbps: 512 bits = 5.12 µs  ⟶ 10× faster wire ⟶ 1/10 the diameter (~250 m)

RULE (from 2.2): a frame's transmission time must exceed 2× the worst-case one-way
propagation delay, so the sender is STILL sending when a far-end collision echoes back.
That is the entire reason for the 64-byte minimum frame — and why higher speed on
shared media means SHORTER allowed distance (unless you enlarge the slot time).
```

### D) 🏗️ The evolution — how collisions were engineered out

```
① COAX BUS (10BASE5 thicknet, 10BASE2 thinnet)
   One physical wire = one collision domain. Every station shares ~10 Mb/s.
   More stations → more collisions → throughput collapses under load.

② HUB = multiport REPEATER (physical star, logical bus)
   Looks like a star, but electrically it just repeats every bit to every port.
   → STILL one collision domain, still half-duplex, still CSMA/CD.
   Bandwidth is SHARED across all ports (10 Mb/s split among everyone).

③ BRIDGE → SWITCH = microsegmentation
   A switch forwards frames only where needed and buffers them.
   → EACH PORT becomes its own collision domain.
   With ONE device per port, there's no one to collide WITH.

④ FULL-DUPLEX (switch port ↔ NIC, dedicated TX/RX pairs)
   Both ends transmit simultaneously on separate wires. Contention gone.
   → CSMA/CD is switched OFF. Collisions are IMPOSSIBLE by construction.
   → Slot-time distance limits vanish (e.g. 100BASE-FX: 412 m half → 2 km full).
   → Full-duplex adds 802.3x PAUSE frames for flow control instead of backoff.
```

> 🧠 **The key insight:** CSMA/CD didn't get "improved" — it got **made irrelevant**. Switching + full-duplex removed the *shared medium* that made collisions possible in the first place. CSMA/CD still lives in the standard for legacy half-duplex, but on a modern switched LAN it simply never runs.

### E) 🔵 The awkward middle: Gigabit half-duplex (802.3z)

```
Problem: at 1 Gb/s, a 64-byte frame takes only ~0.512 µs — collisions could only be
detected across ~25 m. Useless.

Fix #1 — CARRIER EXTENSION: pad the carrier (non-data extension symbols) so the frame
occupies at least 512 BYTES (4096 bit times) on the wire → restores ~200 m diameter.
  Cost: a 64-byte frame drags up to 448 extension bytes of pure waste → awful small-packet
  efficiency (barely better than Fast Ethernet).

Fix #2 — FRAME BURSTING: after the first (extended) frame, a station may send more frames
back-to-back up to a BURST LIMIT of 8192 bytes, filling the IFGs with extension symbols so
it never releases the medium → amortizes the carrier-extension waste.

Reality: almost nobody deploys Gigabit half-duplex. And 10 GbE / 25G / 40G / 100G+ are
FULL-DUPLEX ONLY — CSMA/CD and half-duplex were removed from the spec entirely.
```

### F) 🩺 Duplex mismatch — the CSMA/CD fingerprint you WILL see

```
One end FULL-duplex, other end HALF-duplex (bad autoneg, or one side hardcoded):
  • HALF side thinks it's shared → runs CSMA/CD → sees the full side's mid-frame traffic
    as collisions → reports COLLISIONS, LATE COLLISIONS, runts, FCS errors.
  • FULL side never listens for collisions → happily transmits → sees CRC/FCS errors,
    dropped frames.
Symptom: link is UP and "works," but is dramatically slow and gets WORSE under load.
Fix: make both ends match — ideally BOTH autonegotiate, or BOTH hardcoded identically.
(Full cable/interface fault-finding → CCNA interface & cable issues topic, 1.4.)
```

## 7️⃣ Pros & Cons ⚖️

**CSMA/CD as a mechanism:**

| ✅ Pros | ❌ Cons |
|---|---|
| 🟢 **No central controller** — fully distributed, cheap, self-organizing. | 🔵 **Non-deterministic** — no latency guarantee; a station can be unlucky for many attempts. |
| 🟢 **Simple & robust** on cheap shared coax — a huge reason Ethernet won. | 🔵 **Throughput collapses under load** — more stations ⇒ more collisions ⇒ efficiency drops (often usable ≲ 40–50% on a busy shared segment). |
| 🟢 **Graceful-ish degradation** via adaptive backoff. | 🔵 **Distance-bound by slot time** — higher speed shrinks the network diameter. |
| 🟢 **Backward-compatible** — still in the standard for half-duplex gear. | 🔵 **Capture effect** — one station can unfairly monopolize the medium under contention. |

**Full-duplex switching (what replaced it):**

| ✅ Pros | ❌ Cons |
|---|---|
| 🟢 **No collisions, ever** — deterministic, full line rate each direction. | ⚠️ Needs a **switch** (or direct link) + matching duplex; a **hub can't** do full-duplex. |
| 🟢 **Distance limits from slot time disappear.** | ⚠️ Congestion moves into **switch buffers** → needs flow control (PAUSE) / QoS instead. |
| 🟢 **Each port = private bandwidth** (microsegmentation). | ⚠️ **Duplex mismatch** becomes a new, sneaky failure mode. |

## 8️⃣ Variants — Common → Specific 🌿

**Media-access / duplex modes:**
- 🟢 **Full-duplex, switched** — today's universal default. CSMA/CD idle.
- 🟢 **Half-duplex, shared (hub/coax)** — legacy; CSMA/CD active. Rare now (some IoT/industrial, old gear).
- 🔵 **Gigabit half-duplex** — carrier extension + frame bursting. Almost never seen.

**Access-method family (context — where CSMA/CD sits):**
- 🟢 **CSMA/CD** — wired Ethernet, *detect* collisions after they start.
- 🔵 **CSMA/CA** — Wi-Fi (802.11): *avoid* collisions (can't detect while transmitting on radio). Different animal, same "listen first" ancestry. (→ Block 8 Wireless.)
- 🔵 **Token-based (Token Ring / FDDI)** — collision-free by passing a token; lost the market to cheap switched Ethernet.

**Devices along the evolution:**
- 🔵 **Repeater / Hub** — one collision domain, half-duplex.
- 🔵 **Buffered distributor** (Gigabit "full-duplex repeater") — a transitional hub-like device that buffers to cut collisions.
- 🟢 **Bridge / Switch** — collision-domain-per-port; the winner (→ 2.4).

## 9️⃣ Devices / Media / Protocols 🧰

**Devices & their collision-domain behavior:**
- 🔵 **Hub / repeater** — extends a single collision domain; forwards every bit everywhere; forces half-duplex.
- 🟢 **[Switch](./devices/switch.md) / bridge** — **breaks** the collision domain per port; enables full-duplex; *floods broadcasts* (still one **broadcast** domain per VLAN → 2.6).
- 🟢 **[Router / L3 switch](./devices/router.md)** — bounds both collision and broadcast domains.
- 🟢 **[Endpoint NIC](./devices/nic.md#endpoint-nic)** — runs CSMA/CD only when negotiated to half-duplex; otherwise full-duplex.

**Media:** coax (10BASE5/2, historical), twisted pair & fiber (Block 1). Note: twisted-pair/fiber use **separate TX/RX pairs**, which is *what enables full-duplex* in the first place.

**Protocols / control:**
- 🔵 **Autonegotiation** — negotiates speed & **duplex**; mismatches here cause the fault in §6F.
- 🔵 **802.3x PAUSE (flow control)** — full-duplex replacement for "backoff": tell the far end to pause instead of colliding.
- 🟢 **[FCS/CRC-32](./terms/ethernet-frame-fields.md#fcs)** — the error check that flags the corruption a mismatch produces (from 2.2).

## 🔟 Best Practices ✅

- 🟢 **Design for full-duplex switching end-to-end.** No hubs. One device per access port. This alone eliminates collisions.
- 🟢 **Keep both ends' duplex consistent.** Best practice: let **both** sides autonegotiate. If you must hardcode, **hardcode both** identically (never auto on one side, fixed on the other).
- 🔵 **Read collision counters as diagnosis, not weather.** *Any* `collisions` on a link you believe is full-duplex ⇒ investigate duplex. `late collision` ⇒ duplex mismatch or over-length segment, essentially always.
- 🔵 **Baseline your interface counters** so you can spot `runts`/`late collisions`/`FCS` climbing after a change.
- 🔵 **Retire half-duplex** wherever you find it (old IP phones, legacy sensors, forgotten hubs) — it caps a link far below its rated speed under load.
- 🔵 **Prefer switch buffering + QoS/flow control** to manage congestion in a full-duplex world (the modern analog of backoff).

## 1️⃣1️⃣ No-Goes — Common Mistakes & Troubleshooting Traps 🚫

- ❌ **"A hub is just a small switch."** No — a hub is **one collision domain** (repeats every bit to every port, half-duplex). A switch is **one collision domain per port** (buffers & forwards selectively). Night and day.
- ❌ **Expecting collisions on a modern LAN.** On full-duplex switched links, collisions are **impossible**. If you *see* them, you've found a **fault** (duplex mismatch / a lurking hub), not normal operation.
- ⚠️ **Auto on one side, hardcoded on the other.** Classic duplex-mismatch generator: the auto side can't detect the peer's duplex and **defaults to half** → slow, error-ridden link that still shows "up/up."
- ⚠️ **Confusing collision domain with broadcast domain.** Switches split **collision** domains but **flood broadcasts** — all switch ports (in a VLAN) stay one **broadcast** domain. Only a **router/VLAN boundary** splits broadcast domains (→ 2.6).
- ⚠️ **Reading `late collision` as "just collisions."** Normal collisions happen *early* (within slot time) and are recoverable. **Late** collisions are illegal and **not retried** → real data loss; hunt the duplex/length cause.
- 🔵 **Assuming Gigabit runs CSMA/CD.** Gigabit is virtually always **full-duplex** (no CSMA/CD). The half-duplex carrier-extension machinery is a spec curiosity you'll almost never meet — and **10G+ removed it outright**.
- ❌ **Thinking backoff "fixes" congestion.** Backoff only *resolves collisions*; under heavy load a shared segment still degrades badly. The real fix was **switching**, not a better backoff.

## 1️⃣3️⃣ Practical Tasks 🛠️

> Listed briefly; expanded step-by-step later in context.

1. 🟢 **Count collision domains.** Draw a topology with 1 hub (4 PCs) vs 1 switch (4 PCs). State how many collision domains and broadcast domains each has, and why.
2. 🟢 **Hub vs switch in Packet Tracer 8.** Build both, run continuous pings from several PCs at once, and compare behavior/latency under load. Observe collision LEDs on the hub.
3. 🔵 **Provoke a duplex mismatch (lab).** On two directly-connected switches (GNS3 or real gear), set one interface `duplex full` and the other `duplex half`; generate traffic; watch `show interfaces` grow `collisions`, `late collision`, `runts`, and CRC errors. Fix and confirm they stop.
4. 🔵 **Read the CSMA/CD counters.** On a Cisco device: `show interfaces <id>` → locate `collisions`, `late collision`, `runts`, `input errors`, `output errors`; map each back to a step in §6A.
5. 🔵 **Verify duplex/speed negotiation.** `show interfaces <id> status` and `show interfaces <id>` → confirm `full-duplex`; force half with `duplex half` and observe CSMA/CD "wake up" (collision counters become possible again).
6. 🔵 **Backoff simulation (pen & paper or script).** For 2 stations colliding 3 times in a row, enumerate the possible backoff slot picks each attempt (`{0,1} → {0..3} → {0..7}`) and show when they de-synchronize.
